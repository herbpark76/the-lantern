# Chapter 2: Halmae at the Gate

### 1

It was after the New Year that year when Jeongwon first learned the word *yangdan*.

On New Year's Eve her *Imo* had untied a bundle of holiday clothes, and inside was *jeogori* cloth of *yangdan* meant for her uncle's wife, *oesukmo*. The fabric had a sheen to it, with tiny flowers faintly floating on the surface. Jeongwon laid it over the back of her hand. It was cool and smooth, like resting a hand on water.

"This here's what they call *yangdan*. It's silk, but the pattern's woven right in. Somebody like me, I'd be ashamed to the cloth to wear it."

*Imo* said it with a laugh, but Jeongwon found a different face coming to her mind. It was her grandmother's, in Daeyeon-dong in Busan.

Mother had several *durumagi*. Ramie, silk, and a winter one stuffed with cotton all lay folded in the wardrobe, and each season they were aired once in the sun and folded away again. Grandmother had none. When Jeongwon had seen her last vacation, she was wearing a cotton skirt and *jeogori* with the old man's vest on top. The cuffs of that vest were worn through. It was not that Grandmother treasured it. She looked like a person who did not even know how to treasure things. That had weighed on Jeongwon for a long time.

That night she knelt at the door of the main room.

"Mother, wouldn't it be all right to make Halmae a *durumagi*? Gray *yangdan* with flowers on it. It would look so fine on her."

Mother did not lift her eyes from her sewing.

"Your grandmother has no need of a *durumagi*. The old man will see to it, if she does."

"Halbae doesn't know about such things."

"A girl poking her nose into grown folks' business."

That was the end of it. But Jeongwon knew it was not an end but a beginning. With Mother, a thing that could not be had by asking once could, after a hundred askings, slip over to the side of things that were not impossible. Mother did not listen to what people said, but she could not bear the same words repeated in the same spot until they wore away. Jeongwon was a child who had found that gap. So the next morning, and the evening after, she said the very same words in the very same place.

January went and February went. Haze rose over the Sincheon embankment, and the apricot blossoms opened and fell. Three or four times a month Jeongwon knelt at the door of the main room. She never asked Mother to name a day for going to buy the cloth. She only talked about Halmae's *durumagi*. One day she described the flower pattern, and another day she talked about how the collar band, the *dongjeong*, might be attached. It did not matter that Mother never answered. Jeongwon knew she was listening, because the spacing of the needle piercing the cloth changed, just slightly.

When May came, near Dano, Mother asked.

"Have you got money?"

"No."

"Then what are you going to do?"

"You make it for her, Mother."

Mother said nothing for a long while. Then she stuck her needle into the cloth and said:

"I won't have it said I couldn't even make my mother-in-law one *durumagi*."

Jeongwon never knew where those words came from. It was certain Mother did not say them out of fondness for her mother-in-law. Still, once they were spoken, the matter was settled. Mother did not fetch a length of *yangdan* from Seomun Market. She had the cloth merchant brought to the house, had him spread out his bolts, and made her choice there. It took half a day to pick out the gray with flowers laid on it. In taste, no one could match Mother. That was exactly what Jeongwon had noticed. Mother was a person without warmth, but not a person without skill. And sometimes the skill of a person without warmth carried farther than the heart of a person with it. The garment was finished in time for the vacation three months later, wrapped in *hanji* and tied with a cord. When Jeongwon pressed the layers with her fingertips, they were smooth and straight.

Mother handed her the bundle without adding a word. Jeongwon did not say thank you either. It was not that no such words were needed between them. It was that there was no road for them to travel.

### 2

That summer's trip to Busan was made alone from beginning to end.

Until the year before, a grown-up had gone with her. In the summer she became a fifth-grader, Grandfather sent a letter. She could come on her own now. If Jeongwon bought the train ticket and sent word, he would meet her at Busan Station. The ink on the *hanji* skipped here and there where the brush ran dry, and at the end was written, "From the old man." Mother snorted when she read it. But she did not forbid it.

Her older sister and younger sister did not go. No one had ever asked them to, and they had never asked to go. Jeongwon never learned the reason in all her life. Only when she was grown did she sometimes think this: that in Grandmother's house they had apparently been waiting for Jeongwon alone. If there is only one person waiting, there is only one person to go.

The waiting room at Daegu Station was damp with the smell of people. Cheongdo-daek carried the bundle and followed Jeongwon out. In it were the *yakgwa* and *yugwa* ordered in Gyeongju, the octopus *Imo* had sent, and the *durumagi* bundle. Mother was a person who never skipped the formalities even for a gift. What was to be offered to her husband's parents she prepared one by one, as if measuring it with a ruler. There was no warmth in it, but there was no fault to find either.

"Miss, you go and eat good and come back. *Ajimae*'s heard plenty of talk that the food in that house is the finest in all the land."

"*Ajimae*, what should I bring you when I come back?"

Cheongdo-daek looked away for a moment.

"Bring? What's there to bring. Just go and come back safe."

The slow train let out one long breath of smoke and began to move. The seats were velvet. Worn down and worn down, what had once been green had faded to a yellowish pale green. Jeongwon sat by the window with the *durumagi* bundle in her lap. The midday July sun came in through the window and she watched it raise the short pile of the velvet, strand by strand.

The train stopped everywhere. It stopped at Gyeongsan, at Cheongdo, at Miryang. Different people got on and off at every station. A woman carrying a basket on her head, a man with a child perched on his bundle, an old man in army trousers and rubber shoes. At Samnangjin the salted-fish sellers swarmed aboard. The briny stink of shrimp, anchovy and cutlassfish-gut jars rose all at once, and the carriage turned into the middle of the sea. Jeongwon did not dislike that smell. The apple seller walked through with strings of ten or twelve apples bound in straw. When he came, Jeongwon picked her apples one by one with her eyes. She bought only those with glossy skins and green stems, and anything bruised or shriveled she waved away however cheap he offered it. The seller clicked his tongue as he passed.

"Young as she is, sharp as a tack."

Of the apples she bought that way, the very best was always Grandfather's, the next best Grandmother's. Jeongwon's was whatever was left. It was no kind of rule. It just came to be so.

On the stretch where the Nakdong River showed outside the window, it rained for a short while. While the shower passed, the air in the carriage went suddenly cool and soft. Cozy. Years later, when she could name that feeling in a foreign tongue, Jeongwon would love the word all her life. But then it was nameless, only a warmth in her chest. The more this train ran, the closer she came to Grandfather, and the closer she came to Grandmother, and in that house waited the things Mother had never once handed her. That thought alone was enough to let her sit holding her seat until the people standing had all got off.

### 3

At Busan Station the sea wind was saltier than at Daegu.

Getting off the train and walking among the crowd toward the ticket gate, Jeongwon was up on tiptoe. On the other side of the gate stood a tall man. He had his neck stretched long toward the sky and one hand raised high above the heads of the crowd, waving. Jeongwon could guess how long he had been standing there. There had been an announcement that the train was two hours late, so he must have stood at least that long. Yet he was smiling brightly, like someone who had only just arrived.

Grandfather wore a suit even in summer. A short-sleeved white shirt with no necktie, and trousers tailored of English wool whose crease stood like a blade. Among the old men who came from Daegu to see the blossoms, or among the people of Busan, such height and such dash were rare. Since she was little, Jeongwon had been proud of that dash. She wanted to tell each of the people coming through the gate that this gentleman was her grandfather.

"*Halbae*!"

"Well, well, our Jeongwon's come."

Grandfather took the bundle from her hand and looked her over once, from her head to her shoes, the way one inspects a pack that has come off a long road. Then he said:

"Haven't you grown! You've gotten bigger since I saw you."

"I haven't. I'm the same as ever."

"To your grandfather's eyes you've grown."

Taxis stood in a line in front of the station. Jeongwon knew this did not suit her grandparents' way of living. The two of them did not ordinarily ride taxis. Rich old man though he was, he was careful with every coin, and where he could walk he walked, and where he could take a bus he took a bus. Yet on the days his granddaughter came, they called a taxi. The leather seat in the cab was hot, and outside the window the streets of Busan slid slowly backward. She caught a glimpse of the sea off Yeongdo, and the cranes of the docks hung against the sky.

"Did you study hard and keep well?"

"Yes."

"What rank did you take on this term's exam?"

"First."

"That's right, that's right. Well done." Grandfather nodded, and after a little he asked, "Did your mother give you any scolding?"

Jeongwon could not answer. That question was not cut from the same cloth as the others. The others had been about his granddaughter's marks and her well-being, and this one alone was about his granddaughter's mother. And it neither slighted her nor took her part. It was a word that carefully felt its way inside. From within that question, Jeongwon could not tell whether Grandfather was looking at his son's wife or at his granddaughter.

So she brought out the question she had long held in her heart. Maybe it was a question that could only be asked in this taxi.

"*Halbae*, I don't know if I should ask this. Why does Mother dislike *Gomo* so much? I like *Gomo*."

Grandfather's hand moved, just slightly, on his knee.

"That's how people live."

"Then why is it so?"

"You'll know when you're grown. When you're grown you'll come to know that too, and someday you'll know it all. You're still too young to know."

As he said this he did not look at Jeongwon. He looked out the window. The muscles in the cheek of his profile moved finely. It was a sad face. Jeongwon never forgot that face all her life. It was not a sorrow an eleven-year-old girl could read, but even if she could not read it, she could engrave it. And what is engraved is not erased by the years.

Since she was small, Jeongwon had had a secret suspicion. She wondered whether Mother might be a stepmother. Everyone in the world doted on her, and only Mother treated her differently. It seemed so strange that at some point she had even come to think so. But looking at Grandfather's face reflected in the taxi window, she thought for the first time of another possibility. This may not have begun with me. It may be something that began before me, before I was even born.

### 4

Daeyeon-dong was not the city then.

The taxi turned onto a road not yet paved with asphalt and rattled along it for a long while, until it stopped at a place where the sea opened out in front and a low hill wrapped around the side. The house was not big. Rich as the family was, it had none of the grandeur of the Heo house in Daegu. But in Jeongwon's eyes it was a palace. Perhaps not because the house was a palace, but because the person standing before that house was building one.

Grandmother was standing at the gate.

Jeongwon saw at a glance that Grandmother had been standing there since long before the car stopped. Ever since she'd heard that the train was delayed, she must not have been able to sit still inside the house, and had come out past the brushwood gate to look down the road. A white handkerchief was clutched in her hand. When Jeongwon opened the taxi door and got out, Grandmother put the handkerchief to her eyes. She wiped, and wiped, and wiped again. No matter how she wiped, the tears welled up.

"*Halmae*!"

Jeongwon flew to her without even setting down her bag. Grandmother's arms were soft and firm. Her cotton *jeogori* smelled of grass dried in the sun and of boiled grain. Jeongwon buried her face in Grandmother's chest and cried. She did not know why she cried. She was only sorrowful that this embrace existed, and the sorrow was itself a joy. In eleven years she had not once been held in Mother's arms, and now that she was held, so this is what it is like, the thought crept in. But she did not say those words aloud, not even to herself.

"Ah, my baby. You've had a hard trip coming. You're hungry, aren't you? I've steamed corn."

Grandmother stroked Jeongwon's back down. Over the embrace in which Jeongwon was held, Grandfather's big hand came up and stroked her head once. The two of them said nothing. Grandfather was known as the man who loved his wife best in all the world, and even in old age that love was unchanged. Even to take his wife's hand he was too bashful in front of others. That love was now gathering in one place, through their granddaughter.

A big pot hung in the yard. When the lid was lifted the steam burst up, and out of it came the corn. It was glutinous corn with kernels packed tight. Bite one and it was sweet and chewy, smelling of barley. Grandmother had been steaming it since the word of Jeongwon's coming had reached her. She had steamed a pot every third day, and put on another to fit the day Jeongwon arrived. Once she heard that Jeongwon liked corn, she never forgot it. In the old days people mostly expressed love through food. Not for lack of words, but because food is what cannot be said in words.

Beside the corn lay roasted sweet potatoes, boiled potatoes and steamed eggplant. In one corner of the yard were a sesame patch and a sweet potato patch, and beyond the wall stood rows of corn stalks. Grandfather grew these without using any chemicals. After living as a great landowner in the countryside, he had disposed of nearly all his land and come down to Busan, and the old man's pastime now was to till even a palm-sized plot with devotion.

Around the back of the house was a cliff. That there was a cliff inside the fence was, Jeongwon thought, the strangest and best thing about this house. Water from the small hill above the cliff ran down over the rocks. It was not a great flow. But you could hear it striking the stones, and when the drops sprang up in the sunlight a rainbow stood for a moment. The instant she saw it, Jeongwon thought: I've arrived. It was the feeling of having reached, at eleven, a place one would have to run for a dozen years to get to.

### 5

The supper table was a big one. In that house the table had never once been small.

There was a place for Jeongwon at the edge. Grandfather sat at the head, Grandmother at the foot, looking over the table. There were only three of them, but what was laid out on it would have been more than enough for ten. Each dish was set down so that none overlapped, and even the place for each little soy sauce dish was different.

Everyone got a piece of fish. It was either sea bream or mackerel. That day it was sea bream. It had been grilled over charcoal, the skin golden and the flesh beneath as white as snow. At least one dish was always a seasoned vegetable. Bean sprouts, spinach, or *chwinamul*, and that evening it was *chwinamul*. The steamed egg had puffed up over the earthen pot, and when you touched a spoon to it the cloud-like inside showed. What went into it, Jeongwon never found out in all her life. The squid was stir-fried, or blanched and sliced like raw fish. In Daegu beef usually came to the table, but in this house meat was scarce, and Jeongwon liked vegetables better than meat anyway.

Three or four kinds of kimchi came out separately too. Perilla-leaf kimchi, chive kimchi, young-radish kimchi, and *baek-kimchi*. *Baek-kimchi* was a food too early in the season for July, but when Grandmother heard that Jeongwon was coming, she had put up a jar of it ahead of time. When Jeongwon put it in her mouth it gave a sharp sting. It fizzed like soda. She held a piece in her mouth and closed her eyes. Later she would spend her whole life searching for that taste and never find it again. In the foreign land, even when she made it with similar ingredients, that sting did not come.

Grandmother's food was mild. It was not salty, and even what was spicy was not so spicy as to make your eyes fly open. A person eating it for the first time might call it bland. But after two spoonfuls, three, you felt the ingredients giving up their flavor without hiding it. It was food that let the ingredients speak for themselves, not food that covered the taste with a heavy hand at seasoning. If Mother's food was food to eat with the eyes, Grandmother's was food to eat with the tongue and the belly.

"*Halmae*, how come this steamed egg is so soft?"

"What's so special about it? I didn't put anything in."

"You're lying."

Grandmother only laughed.

When the meal was over Jeongwon went into the room and brought out the bundle wrapped in *hanji*. She held it up in both hands and set it down in front of Grandmother.

"*Halmae*, open this."

"What is it?"

"Open it and you'll see."

Grandmother wiped her hands and undid the cord. When she opened the *hanji*, the gray *yangdan* showed itself. The flower pattern was subtle, and when she held it up to the oil lamp the light swept off to one side. Grandmother gazed at it a long while, then drew back her hand without having touched it.

"Ah, how can it be so lovely. Whose is this?"

"It's yours, *Halmae*. Try it on."

"How could I wear a thing like this? An old woman like me."

"You have to try it now, *Halmae*. I pestered Mother six months for it."

Grandfather let out a hearty laugh. Grandmother draped the *durumagi* over her shoulders. At first she fussed awkwardly with the sleeves, but soon she fastened the cord and tied the *goreum*. When the gray *yangdan* wrapped around Grandmother's white hair and straight brow, a picture stood there. In her heart Jeongwon cried out: See? What did I tell you? Didn't I say this would suit Halmae?

Grandmother did not say it aloud, but her eyes were full of tears. She did not take out her handkerchief to wipe them. She just let them be.

"Your mother must have been worn out. How hard our Jeongwon must have pestered her."

"No, Mother made it herself."

As she said so, a corner of Jeongwon's heart ached. She knew that Mother had made it not for her mother-in-law but for appearances. But she did not say that. Grandmother probably knew too. Knowing, yet pretending not to, she spent two days wearing that garment. On the third day she wore it all the way out to the old people's hall at the mouth of the village. There was not another old person in the neighborhood in a *durumagi* like it. That evening Jeongwon watched Grandmother come home having boasted to her friends without saying a word. A boast that showed without being spoken, Jeongwon thought, was the most dignified boast in the world.

The joy Jeongwon felt at seeing Grandmother in that garment was probably greater than Grandmother's own. The joy of the one who receives is great, but there are times when the joy of the one who gives is greater. That secret, an eleven-year-old girl learned that day, and she never threw it away as long as she lived.

### 6

The next morning opened with the smell of *jaecheopguk*.

Before the dawn broke there was a clattering from the kitchen. Jeongwon lay quietly under the quilt and listened. It was Buhui. She was a girl of sixteen or seventeen who worked in the house, learning cooking under Grandmother. Jeongwon did not know her family name, only that her name was Buhui. She was small, with round eyes, and when she smiled a dimple dug itself into one cheek. The poor children Jeongwon had seen in Daegu were mostly dark and gloomy in the face, but Buhui was different. A poor family's daughter, yet no shadow lay on her face. Jeongwon could not tell where that came from, but when the girl was near, her breathing strangely eased.

The *jaecheopguk* was a soup with a heap of marsh clams in each bowl and beaten egg stirred in, boiled thick. Every morning Jeongwon ate a bowlful. One spoonful and her insides loosened and cleared. It was less a soup than a greeting the sea offered in the morning.

"Miss, what'll you do today?"

"Buhui, I want to go see the sea."

The two went down to the shore. The tide was out on the mudflat in front of the fisheries college. Behind it she could see the green ridge of the UN Cemetery. As she stepped, clamshells crunched underfoot. Jeongwon liked the feel, so she walked slowly on purpose. Then she felt something pop up beneath her foot. Looking down, it was a clam. A fat hard clam was poking its head out of the sand. One step further and another popped up. A clam came up with every step. The ground under her feet was a whole field of clams.

"Buhui, there's a mess of clams here."

"There are, aren't there."

"If we took these home and made Halmae a soup, how nice that'd be. With fresh ones."

Jeongwon looked at Buhui's face after she said it. It was a word she already felt a little guilty about. If Buhui had said no, she would just have backed off. But Buhui said, her eyes shining:

"Let's do it. Together."

So the two bent at the waist and picked up clams. When they had gathered about ten, Jeongwon's skirt hung heavy. They hurried home, and only after they got there did it come out of Buhui's mouth that the mudflat was where the fisheries college had planted clams for an experiment.

"Miss, they say that place belongs to the school. That they planted them on purpose for an experiment."

"You fool, you're telling me *now*?"

"But you said to do it, Miss."

"You're the one who said let's do it!"

The two looked at each other and burst out laughing. The laughter was mixed with fear. It was theft. It was the first theft of Jeongwon's life. And a girl who had once heard the story of the teacher's bundle and said the world was truly filthy had now carried off, without a word, something belonging to someone else. Her heart pounded. Though no one was chasing her, she looked back over her shoulder.

Grandmother took the clams from them in the kitchen, looked at them once, and said:

"Didn't Buhui tell you? Those were put there for an experiment."

"I didn't know, *Halmae*."

"Well, if you'd known you'd have put them back. What's to be done now? Having been dug up already, we'd best eat them."

With a wry smile Grandmother poured in water and set the clams boiling. She put tofu and scallions into the clear broth, and at the very last laid a lightly beaten egg over the top. It had a mild taste, not salty. The clam meat loosened softly in the mouth. Grandfather took one spoonful and chuckled.

"Well, well, it's good you dug them up."

Jeongwon looked up at Grandfather, spoon in hand. He had not scolded the granddaughter who had stolen. He had praised her instead. In the Daegu house such a thing was unimaginable. Mother would have stabbed a finger at Jeongwon's forehead on the spot, and for days on end she would have gone on about what would happen if word got out that a Heo daughter laid hands on other people's things. But in this house even theft, once it became soup, became a meal.

"Better by far than the clams from Seomun Market in Daegu. The sea-smell's still alive in them."

Those words were bigger than any comfort. Jeongwon drank down the soup in gulps. The fear in her heart settled, and as the broth went down her throat her whole body warmed.

### 7

One more thing Jeongwon could not forget in that house was the tussle with Buhui over the fish.

Buhui always laid the best piece on Jeongwon's plate and ate the scraps for herself, the tail and the bones. She was a perceptive girl. She was a girl who knew what it meant to wait on the mistress's granddaughter, and Jeongwon knew that it was poverty that had taught her. But Jeongwon could not just stand by and watch it.

"Hey, you eat too."

"No. I mustn't eat things like this."

"Who said you mustn't?"

"I mustn't."

"I'm telling you to eat, so why won't you, you silly girl."

With her chopsticks Jeongwon pulled the thickest meat off the fish and set it on top of Buhui's rice. Buhui, horrified, put it back on Jeongwon's rice. Jeongwon put it on Buhui's again. After this had gone back and forth two or three times, Grandmother, who could stand it no longer, burst out laughing.

"That's right, eat each other's. If you keep giving way to each other the fish'll go cold."

"*Halmae*, Buhui says she won't eat it."

"Buhui, it's Miss's wish. Take it."

Buhui, with reluctance, put the piece in her mouth. Her eyes went round. She could not hide the joy spreading in her mouth. Watching, Jeongwon thought to herself: This girl knows taste too. She knows taste because she's clever. Cooking is a matter of the head. A girl who could do it exactly the same the next day after being shown once, what might she have become if only they'd sent her to school?

That thought darkened Jeongwon's heart for a while that day. In this world, even with the same head, one girl in a velvet coat goes into the teachers' room clutching a bundle, and another eats fish tails in someone else's kitchen. Eleven-year-old Jeongwon thought it unfair. But even if, as *Imo* had taught her, she said that what was unfair was unfair, there was no one to hear it. So she did small things instead. Whenever she had a pretty hairpin or handkerchief she'd picked up somewhere, she handed it to Buhui, saying, "If you want it, take it." Each time Buhui smiled until her eyes were half-moons and said:

"Even if you only come and go now and then, Miss, it's like heaven."

Jeongwon never forgot those words. Heaven, she'd said. Did heaven and hell really exist apart? If there was a place like this anywhere in the world, that was heaven. Jeongwon would say so later, even at seventy-five, thinking of this house.

### 8

At night Jeongwon slept in the middle.

Grandfather and Grandmother spread their bedding in the center of the room and laid Jeongwon between them. From either side she heard the breathing of the two old people, taking turns. At first she lay straight with her body stretched out, awkward, but before long she turned on her side and buried her face against Grandmother. Even at that age, even through middle school and high school, Jeongwon slept in that spot. And sleeping that way, no one was ever ashamed.

On the night she arrived the two of them rubbed her arms and legs. Grandmother took the arms, Grandfather the legs. The train ride had been long, so her whole body must be stiff. The touch of the two old hands was not what a grown-up does for a child. It was how a person with years piled up greets the one who has come before.

"Stop it. Your arms will hurt, *Halmae*. *Halbae*, you stop too."

"Hush now. You've had a hard time coming."

Strangely, that touch pained Jeongwon's heart. These two were old. Their strength had drained, and the joints of their hands were thick. That such hands were kneading a little granddaughter's legs was more than she could bear. She sat up.

"*Halmae*, *Halbae*, I'll rub for you."

"No, you lie down."

"No. I'm going to do it."

As the three of them reached out, each insisting on rubbing the others, the room grew noisy, and at last Grandfather burst into loud laughter. Grandmother laughed along with his laughter, and Jeongwon laughed too. The oil lamp flickered, and on the wall the shadows of three people tangled and danced. Those hands on that night, each trying to rub the other, Jeongwon remembered all her life. Later, in the strange land of America, every time she saw a child cursing at a mother, she thought of the hands of this night. That love is people contending over what they might give to one another, Jeongwon learned in that room.

After the light was out, she heard the waves. *Chulssuk, chulssuk, chulkeodeok.* The deeper the night the louder the sea. With her eyes shut, listening to that sound, Jeongwon thought: Over on the far side, isn't there some little night boat? Isn't someone rowing across those waves toward us? It would be so good to take a night boat anywhere. But where she wanted to go, she did not know. There was nowhere she wanted to go away from this spot. It was only that she liked the word night boat. It seemed to come from far away and also to be going far away, and the word made her heart faint and tender.

Later, nineteen years on and then that many again, in some year of the eighties when she visited this shore again, a popular song of the same name was playing there. Jeongwon stood a long while listening to the song that only shared its name. But that song was not a boy's song, and it was not the sound of the waves of this night. The night boat of this night was not in the song.

Beside her Grandmother's chest rose and fell, regular. Jeongwon tried to breathe in time with that breath. At first they didn't match. Then they matched a little. Then at some moment the two had become one.

### 9

When she boarded the train back to Daegu two weeks later, Jeongwon sat by the window and looked out toward the ticket gate for a long time. Grandfather this time too was stretching his neck toward the sky and waving. Even after the train began to move that hand did not come down. Jeongwon pressed her forehead against the window and watched until the hand became a dot.

In her lap were the corn and sweet potatoes and the jar of perilla-leaf kimchi Grandmother had packed. She had bought a string of apples too. This time she picked the very best for her own share. But once she actually took a bite, there was no sweetness. What remained in her mouth was the aftertaste of the *jaecheopguk* that had not yet faded, the warmth of the two pairs of hands that had kneaded her legs, and Grandfather's sad profile looking out the taxi window.

The train passed Samnangjin. The salted-fish sellers came aboard, and that briny stink filled the carriage. In the midst of that smell Jeongwon closed her eyes. Once this train rounded that one mountain, it would be Daegu. In Daegu there was a house with a big gate, and a yard where not one step was out of place, and a brother whose report card was read twice and herself whose was read once. And in that house's kitchen was Cheongdo-daek. A person whose name no one knew, a person whose name Jeongwon had never asked. Suddenly Jeongwon thought that this vacation she must be sure to ask Cheongdo-daek her name.
