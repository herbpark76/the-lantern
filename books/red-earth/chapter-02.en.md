# Chapter 2: The Grandmother at the Gate

### 1

It was 1962, two days after Seollal, that Jeongwon first learned the word *yangdan*.

Her aunt had come to bow to the elders and untied her bundle, and inside was a length of *jeogori* cloth for Jeongwon's uncle's wife. The fabric had a sheen, with a faint scatter of tiny flowers floating on it. When Jeongwon laid it across the back of her hand, it felt cool and smooth, as if she had rested her hand on water.

"This here's what they call *yangdan*. It's silk, all right, but with the pattern woven right in. Somebody like your aunt, now, I'd only be a shame to the cloth, wearing it."

Her aunt said it laughing, but the words called up an unexpected face in Jeongwon's mind: her grandmother's, in Daeyeon-dong in Busan.

Her mother owned several *durumagi*, the long coats worn over *hanbok*. Ramie ones and plain-silk ones, even a winter one with quilted wadding, all folded away in the wardrobe, aired in the sun once each season and folded again. Her grandmother owned none. When Jeongwon had seen her last vacation, she had been wearing her grandfather's old vest over a cotton *jeogori*, and the cuffs of that vest were worn through. She seemed not to know how to look after herself. It had troubled Jeongwon for a long time.

That night she knelt down in front of the door to the inner room.

"Eomma, couldn't we make Halmae a *durumagi*? Gray *yangdan*, with flowers on it. It'd suit her that well."

Her mother did not raise her eyes from her sewing.

"Your grandmother has no need of a *durumagi*. The old man will see to it, I expect."

"Halbae doesn't know about such things."

"A girl meddling in grown-ups' business."

That was the end of it, but Jeongwon knew it was only the beginning. Her mother never gave in the first time she was pressed, but she could not bear to hear the same words repeated in the same place until they were worn away. Jeongwon was a child who had found that crack. So the next morning, and the evening after that, she said the same words in the same place.

February passed and March passed, haze rose over the Sincheon embankment, and the apricot blossoms opened and fell. Three or four times a month Jeongwon knelt before the door of the inner room. Some days she talked about how the flower pattern would look, some days about how best to attach the *dongjeong*. It didn't matter that there was no reply. She knew her mother was listening, because the intervals at which the needle pierced the cloth had changed, very slightly.

Around Dano, her mother asked:

"Have you got the money?"

"No, Eomma."

"Then what do you mean to do?"

"Please make it for me, Eomma."

Her mother said nothing for a long while. Then she stuck the needle into the cloth.

"I don't want it said that I couldn't so much as make my mother-in-law a *durumagi*."

Jeongwon never knew where those words came from. It was plain that her mother did not say them out of fondness for her mother-in-law. Yet once they were said, the matter was settled. Her mother did not go to the market to cut cloth. She summoned the draper himself to the house and had him spread out his goods. Choosing a gray ground with flowers laid on it took half a day. No one could match her mother's eye. The coat was finished in time for summer vacation two months later and wrapped in *hanji*.

Her mother handed over the bundle without adding a single word, and Jeongwon did not say thank you. Between them such words were not unnecessary. They simply had no road to travel by.

### 2

That summer's trip to Busan was made alone from beginning to end.

In June of the year she became a fifth-grader, a letter had come from Busan. She could come by herself now. If they bought the train ticket and sent it, someone would meet her at Busan Station. The brush-written characters on the *hanji* ran slightly each time the tip of the brush went dry, and at the end it was signed "The Old Man." Her mother snorted when she read the letter, but she did not forbid it.

The grandfather and grandmother in Busan were her father's parents. They were the other side from her maternal grandmother in Uiseong, her mother's mother, where she went every vacation. In Uiseong she stayed with her cousins, but to Busan Jeongwon always went alone. Her older sister and younger sister did not go. No one had ever suggested they go, and neither had ever said she would. Jeongwon never did find out why. Only, once grown, she used to think that in that house one person alone had been waited for.

Her father's youngest sister, Gomo, lived in Busan too: the aunt who had once entered the Miss Korea pageant and spoke as neatly as a schoolteacher. On the days Gomo visited Daegu, Jeongwon's mother grew quieter and more correct, and in her mother, correctness was another name for cold.

The waiting room at Daegu Station was damp with the smell of people. Cheongdo-daek had come all the way to the station, carrying the bundle. In it were *yakgwa* and *yugwa* ordered from Gyeongju, an octopus her aunt had sent, and the *durumagi* wrapped in *hanji*. Her mother had packed what was to be offered to her parents-in-law as if measuring it off with a ruler. There was no warmth in it, but there was no fault to be found in it either.

"*Agassi*, go and eat well, and come back. Ajimae's heard many a time that the food in that house is the finest in all the world."

"Ajimae, what shall I bring you when I come back?"

Cheongdo-daek looked away for a moment.

"Bring? What's there to bring. Just go, and come back safe."

The slow train let out one long breath of smoke and began to move. The seats were velvet, worn so thin that what had been green was now a yellowed pale green. Jeongwon sat by the window with the bundle of the *durumagi* in her lap. The July noon sun made each short nap of the velvet stand on end.

The train stopped at every place. Gyeongsan, Cheongdo, Miryang. At Samnangjin, the salted-fish sellers swarmed aboard, and the briny stink of salted shrimp and salted hairtail innards rose all at once, so that the car became like the middle of the sea. Jeongwon didn't mind the smell. The apple seller carried strings of apples braided in straw, ten or twelve to a string. Jeongwon bought only the ones with glossy skin and green stems, and waved away any with a bruise, however cheap they were offered.

"Little thing's got an eye on her."

Of the apples she bought, the best one was her grandfather's, the next her grandmother's. Jeongwon's own was whatever was left. It wasn't a rule. It had simply come to be so.

Where the Nakdong River came into view, a short shower passed. The air in the car turned suddenly cool and soft. Later, when she was able to name this feeling in a foreign tongue, Jeongwon would come to love that word, "cozy," for years and years; but now, nameless, her chest was simply warm. The farther the train ran, the nearer her grandfather and grandmother came, and in that house were waiting things her mother had never once handed her.

### 3

At Busan Station the sea wind was saltier than at Daegu.

Walking toward the ticket gate, Jeongwon was up on tiptoe. Over there stood a tall man. He had stretched his neck long toward the sky and was waving one hand high above the heads of the crowd. The train had been two hours late, so he must have stood there at least that long, yet he was smiling brightly, like someone who had just arrived.

Her grandfather wore a suit even in summer. A short-sleeved white shirt, no necktie, and trousers tailored from English wool, the crease standing up sharp as a blade. A man of such height and such dash was rare even among the people of Busan. Jeongwon wanted to announce to everyone coming through the gate that this old gentleman was her grandfather.

"Halbae!"

"Oi, our Jeongwon's come."

Her grandfather took the bundle and looked her over once, from her head to her shoes, as if inspecting luggage that had come a long way.

"You've grown some more."

"I haven't. Same as always."

"In Halbae's eyes you're big."

In front of the station a line of taxis stood waiting. The two of them did not ordinarily take taxis. They were rich old folks, but careful with every coin, and where they could walk they walked, where they could go by bus they went by bus. Yet on the day their granddaughter came, they called a taxi. The leather seat was hot, and outside the window the streets of Busan slid slowly backward. Cranes at the docks hung in the sky.

"Did you do well at your studies?"

"Yes."

"What place did you take this exam?"

"First."

"That's right, that's right. Good girl." Her grandfather nodded, and after a little he asked, "Did your mother nag at you?"

Jeongwon could not answer. That question was of a different grain from the others. It wasn't about her marks or her health. It was about her mother.

Jeongwon brought out the question she had long held inside her. It was a question that could be asked only in this taxi.

"Halbae, I don't know if I'm allowed to ask this. But why does Eomma dislike Gomo so much? I like Gomo."

Her grandfather's hand moved, very slightly, on his knee.

"That's how it is with people living."

"Then why is it so?"

"When you're grown you'll know. Someday you'll know all of it. You're too young to understand now."

Her grandfather did not look at her; he looked out the window. A muscle in his cheek, in profile, moved faintly. It was a sad face. It was not a face an eleven-year-old could read. She remembered it anyway.

Jeongwon had a secret suspicion: that her mother might be a stepmother. It was because it seemed so strange that all the world doted on her and only her mother treated her differently. But looking at her grandfather's face reflected in the taxi window, she thought for the first time of another possibility. This may not have begun with me. It may be something that began before I was born.

### 4

Daeyeon-dong in those days was not the city.

The taxi jolted a long while over a road with no asphalt, and stopped at a place where the sea opened out ahead and a low hill held the house in its arm on one side. The house was not large. It had none of the grandeur of the Heo house in Daegu. But to Jeongwon's eyes it was a palace.

Her grandmother was standing at the gate.

Jeongwon knew at a glance that she had been standing there since long before the car stopped. Since word came that the train was late, her grandmother must have been unable to sit inside the house and had come out past the brushwood gate to gaze only at the road. In her hand she clutched a white handkerchief. When Jeongwon got out of the taxi, her grandmother touched the handkerchief to her eyes. She wiped, and wiped, and wiped again. No matter how she wiped, the tears welled up.

"Halmae!"

Jeongwon flew, not even setting down her bag. Her grandmother's embrace was soft and firm. The cotton *jeogori* smelled of sun-dried grass and of grain being boiled. Jeongwon buried her face and wept. She did not know why she wept. In eleven years of living she had never once been held in her mother's arms, and now, being held, a thought crept in: so this is what it's like. But she did not say it, not even aloud inside herself.

"Aigu, my pup. You must've had a hard trip coming. Hungry, aren't you? I've steamed corn."

Her grandmother stroked down Jeongwon's back. Over that came her grandfather's big hand, and it patted Jeongwon's head once. The two of them said nothing. Her grandfather was a man who loved his wife more than anyone in the world, and even in old age that love was so steady that he was bashful about so much as taking her hand before others.

A big pot had been hung over the fire in the yard. When the lid came off the steam shot up, and out came sticky corn with kernels packed tight. It was sweet and chewy when bitten, and it smelled of barley. From the time word came that Jeongwon was coming, her grandmother had steamed a pot every third day, and for the day of arrival had set another on. She had heard once that Jeongwon liked corn and had never forgotten it. In the old days love was mostly expressed through food.

Around the back of the house was a cliff. A cliff inside the fence was, to Jeongwon, the strangest and best thing about that house. Water flowing from the little hill above the cliff came down the rock. It was no great stream, but you could hear it strike the stone, and when the drops sprang up in the sunlight, a rainbow stood for a moment. The instant she saw it, Jeongwon thought: I've arrived.

### 5

The supper table was a big one. In that house a table had never once been small.

There were three of them, but the table was laid for ten to eat their fill and leave some over. One piece of fish for each person, sea bream or mackerel. That day it was sea bream grilled over charcoal. There was always one kind of *namul*, and that day it was *chwi-namul*. The *gyeran-jjim* swelled up over its earthen pot, showing its cloudlike inside, and what had gone into it Jeongwon never did find out. The kimchi was perilla-leaf kimchi, chive kimchi, young-radish kimchi, and white kimchi. White kimchi was early fare for July, but her grandmother had put up a jar in advance when she heard Jeongwon was coming. Put in the mouth, it sparkled like soda pop. Jeongwon closed her eyes with a piece between her teeth. After she married, she never tasted white kimchi like it again.

Her grandmother's food was mild. It was not salty, and the hot was never hot enough to make your eyes pop open. Someone eating it for the first time might have called it bland. But after two spoonfuls, three spoonfuls, you felt the ingredients bringing out their own flavor without hiding it. If her mother's food was food you ate with your eyes, her grandmother's was food you ate with tongue and belly.

"Halmae, how come this *gyeran-jjim*'s so soft?"

"Why, what's so special. I put nothing in it."

"That's a lie."

Her grandmother only laughed.

When the meal was over, Jeongwon fetched the *hanji* bundle from the room, held it in both hands, and set it before her grandmother.

"Halmae, open this."

Her grandmother wiped her hands and undid the cord. The *hanji* opened and the gray *yangdan* came out. The flowers were faint, and when held to the lamplight, the sheen swept off to one side. Her grandmother gazed a long time, then drew back her hand without having touched it.

"Aigu, how come it's so lovely. Who's this for?"

"It's yours, Halmae. Put it on. I pestered Eomma six months."

Her grandfather gave a great laugh. Her grandmother laid the *durumagi* over her shoulders. At first she fussed awkwardly with the sleeves, but soon she tied the ribbons. When the gray *yangdan* wrapped around her white hair and straight brow, a picture stood there. Jeongwon cried inside: See? Didn't I say it would suit Halmae.

Tears had gathered in her grandmother's eyes, but she did not take out the handkerchief to wipe them. She let them be.

"Your mother must've been sorely pestered. How hard you must've pestered, my Won-i."

"No, Eomma made it for me."

Saying so, Jeongwon felt an ache in one corner of her heart. She knew her mother had made it not for her mother-in-law but for appearances. Perhaps her grandmother knew as well. Knowing, and pretending not to, she spent two days in that garment, and on the third day wore it as far as the old folks' hall at the entrance of the village. No other old person in the village wore a *durumagi* so fine. That evening Jeongwon watched her grandmother come home from showing it off to her friends without a word.

The joy Jeongwon felt at seeing her grandmother in the coat was probably greater than her grandmother's own.

### 6

The next morning opened with the smell of *jaecheop* soup.

From before dawn there was a clattering in the kitchen. It was Buhui. She was a girl of sixteen or seventeen who worked in the house and was learning cooking under Jeongwon's grandmother. Jeongwon didn't know her family name, only that her name was Buhui. She was short, with round eyes, and when she smiled a dimple dented one cheek. The poor children Jeongwon had seen in Daegu were mostly dark and gloomy in the face, but Buhui was different. It was easier to breathe around her.

The *jaecheop* soup was piled with clams, and eggs were stirred into it thickly. With one spoonful, the insides loosened and cleared.

"*Agassi*, what will you do today?"

"Buhui, I want to go see the sea."

The mudflat in front of the Fisheries College was bare of water, and behind it you could see the green ridge of the UN Cemetery. As she stepped forward, shell fragments crunched under her feet. Then something popped up beneath her sole. A fat clam was sticking its head out of the sand. One step farther, and another popped. At every step, clams came up.

"Buhui, there's loads of these clams. If we took some home and boiled them for Halmae, wouldn't that be something. Nice fresh ones."

Having said it, Jeongwon looked at Buhui's face. The words already felt a little uneasy inside her. Had Buhui said it wasn't allowed, she would have simply backed off. But Buhui's eyes lit up.

"Let's do it. Together."

By the time they'd gathered ten or so, their skirt-folds were heavy. Only after they got home did it come out of Buhui's mouth that the flat was where the Fisheries College had planted clams for an experiment.

"*Agassi*, they say that's the school's. They say they planted them on purpose, for experiments."

"You dolt, you tell me that now?"

"But it was you who said to, *Agassi*."

"You're the one said 'let's do it,' weren't you."

The two stared at each other and burst out laughing. Fear was mixed into the laughter. It was a kind of theft. Taking what belonged to someone else without a word, their hearts pounded, and they kept glancing back though no one was chasing them.

Her grandmother took the clams in the kitchen, looked at them once, and said:

"Didn't Buhui tell you? Those were set out for an experiment."

"I didn't know, Halmae."

"Aigo, had you known you'd have put them back. What's to be done now. What's been dug has to be eaten."

Her grandmother smiled wryly, poured water, and boiled the clams. Into the clear broth she put tofu and scallions, and at the end gently stirred in an egg. It was not salty, but plain and pure. Her grandfather took one spoonful and gave a hearty laugh.

"Aigo, well dug, well dug."

Jeongwon looked up with her spoon in hand. Her grandfather had praised, not scolded, a granddaughter who had stolen. In the Daegu house it would have been unimaginable. Her mother would have jabbed a finger at her forehead on the spot and gone on about it for days: what if word got out that a daughter of the Heo house had laid hands on what wasn't hers?

"Better than the clams from Seomun Market, this. The smell of the flat's still alive in it."

Jeongwon slurped down the broth. The fear that had been in her settled, and warmth spread through her body.

### 7

There is one more thing Jeongwon cannot forget from that house: the tussle with Buhui over the fish.

Buhui always put the best piece on Jeongwon's plate and ate for herself the tail and the scraps with the bones left on. She was a girl who understood things. She knew what it meant to wait on the granddaughter of the household, and Jeongwon knew it was poverty that had taught her. But she couldn't just watch.

"Hey, you eat too."

"No, miss. I mustn't eat such things."

"Who says you mustn't? I say eat, so why won't you, you silly girl."

With her chopsticks Jeongwon picked off the thickest piece of flesh and laid it on top of Buhui's rice. Buhui, aghast, put it right back on Jeongwon's rice. Jeongwon put it on Buhui's again. After two or three trips back and forth, her grandmother, unable to bear it, couldn't hold in her laughter.

"That's right, keep on at each other. By the time you're done being polite the fish'll be cold."

"Buhui, it's *Agassi*'s heart. Take it."

Buhui put the piece in her mouth, reluctantly. Her eyes went round, and she couldn't hide the joy that spread in her mouth. Jeongwon thought to herself: this one knows taste too. She's clever, that's why she knows taste. Show her once and she does it the same the next day. What would she have become if they had sent her to school?

That thought darkened her heart for a while. With the very same head, one girl has a mother who carries a cloth-wrapped bundle into the teachers' room in a velvet coat, and another eats fish tails in someone else's kitchen. Jeongwon had been taught that when you are wronged you must say you are wronged, or you fall sick. But for this wrong there was no one to listen even if she said it. So instead she did small things. If she had a pretty hairpin or a handkerchief, she handed it over with "Take it if you like." Each time Buhui smiled until her eyes became half moons.

"*Agassi*, even if you only come now and then, it's like heaven."

Heaven and hell aren't somewhere else, Jeongwon says now, at seventy-five, remembering that house. A place like that, right here in this world, is heaven.

### 8

At night Jeongwon slept in the middle, between the two of them.

In the center of the room a mattress was laid, and her grandfather and grandmother lay Jeongwon down between them. From either side came the breathing of the two old people, taking turns. She did not leave that spot, even after she went to middle school, even after high school.

When the lamp was put out, she could hear the waves. *Cheolssok, cheolssok, cheolkeodeok.* The deeper the night, the louder the sea. Jeongwon closed her eyes and thought. Might there not be, somewhere on the far side, some small night boat? Might someone not be rowing across those waves toward here? It would be so good to go off in a night boat. But where she wanted to go, she did not know. There was nowhere she wanted to go away from this spot. It was only that she liked the words "night boat." They seemed to come from far away and to go far away, and they made her heart ache faintly.

Then, out of the dark, two hands came. Her grandmother took the arms, her grandfather the legs. It had been so from the first day.

"You must be sore all over from the train. Hold still."

Those hands made her heart ache. The two were old, their strength gone, the joints of their fingers thick. That such hands should be kneading a little granddaughter's legs was more than she could bear, and Jeongwon sat up.

"Halmae, Halbae, let me knead for you."

"No, you lie down."

"No. I'll do it."

The three of them reached out, each insisting on kneading the others, until the room was in an uproar, and at last her grandfather burst into great laughter. Her grandmother laughed along, and so did Jeongwon. The lamp flickered and the shadows of the three danced entangled on the wall. Later, in America, whenever she saw a child curse its mother, Jeongwon would think of the hands of that night.

When she lay down again, her grandmother's chest rose and fell regularly beside her. Jeongwon tried breathing in time with that sound. At first they did not match. Then they matched a little. Then, without her noticing, they matched.

### 9

On the train back to Daegu fifteen days later, Jeongwon sat by the window and looked out toward the ticket gate for a long time. Her grandfather again had his neck stretched toward the sky, waving. Even when the train moved, that hand did not come down. Jeongwon pressed her forehead against the glass and watched until the hand became a dot.

In her lap were the corn and sweet potatoes and the jar of perilla-leaf kimchi her grandmother had packed. She had bought a string of apples too. This time she picked the very best one for herself. But even after biting into it, there was no sweetness. What remained in her mouth was the lingering aftertaste of the *jaecheop* soup, the warmth of the two hands that had kneaded her legs, and her grandfather's sad profile as he looked out the window of the taxi.

The train passed Samnangjin. The salted-fish sellers came aboard and the briny reek filled the car. Jeongwon closed her eyes in that smell. One more hill and it was Daegu. In Daegu was the house with the big gate, the yard where not one step was out of order, and the mother who read her brother's report card twice and Jeongwon's once. And in the kitchen of that house was Cheongdo-daek. A person whose name no one knew, a person whose name Jeongwon had never asked. Suddenly Jeongwon thought: this time I must ask.

The train was slowly entering the dark. It seemed that somewhere a nameless night boat, rowing along, was keeping pace beside this train.

### 10

An afternoon in autumn 2026, in Dallas.

In the sunlight lying long across the living-room floor, Sophie lay at Jeongwon's feet with her chin on her paws. A small light was lit on the recorder. Seventy-five-year-old Jeongwon, leaning back against the sofa, said nothing for a long time, then drew a blanket up to her knees and began.

"Halmae was standing at the gate. With a handkerchief. And I flew to her, Halmae! just like that."

As she spoke, the corners of her eyes grew a little damp. Sophie raised her head, looked up once, and lay back down. Today again her daughter would telephone from Sydney and ask whether she had eaten. Jeongwon thought of the lunch she had not eaten. For one bowl of that white kimchi she wouldn't begrudge even a thousand dollars.

"That vacation, you know. I asked Cheongdo-daek her name."

Sophie's ears pricked up. Outside the window, the wind was moving through the live oaks.
