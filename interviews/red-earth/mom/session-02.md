# Interview session 02: Warm-up: the books she loves (Toji, Taebaek Sanmaek)

- **Subject:** Mom
- **Date:** 2026-10-10 (46 min, 15 answers)
- **Story:** red-earth

## Summary
- **Session context:** The topic was meant to be her favorite books, but she moved quickly from *Toji* to her own childhood in Daegu and her extended family. At the end she asked what theme the interviewer saw in her stories. She settled on "warmth from outsiders in a family without 정 (affection)".
- **Toji and Taebaek Sanmaek:**
  - Most memorable in *Toji*: 별당아씨 (Byeoldang Lady) and her lover (she can't recall his name) escaping together and living happily. She also remembers the grandmother-figure who kept leading the big household after her husband died (character unclear).
  - She says she read it "about 10 years ago". In Session 1 she said she hadn't read it for about 5 years, so the timing is unclear.
  - The banquets, crowds and many servants reminded her of her own 친정집 (childhood home); it felt beautiful, sad and nostalgic.
  - She prefers *Toji* because it is about family. She finds *Taebaek Sanmaek* more ideological.
- **Her mother vs. the mother in Toji:**
  - She says she does not respect her own mother ("in my heart") or her father. She does feel respect for the mother in *Toji*, who let the couple go for their happiness.
  - She believes her own mother would have sent her to a far island and killed her if she had run off with someone (said as a vivid exaggeration, we think).
  - She never once remembers being held in her mother's arms. She felt like her mother's "rival" and says her mother did not care for her.
  - Her mother was a 경북여고 (Kyungpook Girls' High School) graduate from the Japanese colonial era. She was proud, an advisor to relatives on marriages, a good cook in her own eyes, and fond of showing off. She disliked her in-laws, and the house was cold and "stiff", with her parents always fighting.
  - Later her maternal aunt's family went bankrupt, while her mother worked hard and became "the richest in Daegu".
- **Siblings:**
  - She is the middle of three daughters, with an older sister, a younger sister and (apparently) a brother, probably older (unclear). She was the top student at 경북여고 and says she felt a little ashamed that her sister and younger sister were less bright.
  - She believes her sisters resented her because everyone loved her.
  - She heard as a child that a girl is "not worth the dirt on a brother's hoof", the root of her hatred of unfairness. She says it left a lasting 한 (accumulated sorrow). With her two dogs she deliberately favors the less-favored one.
  - Her older sister loved money and stole the money her grandparents had given her. Her mother took the sister's side.
- **Education:** Her homeroom teachers at 경북여고 said she could enter any department at Ewha Womans University. Her mother, though wealthy and "high class", refused, and the homeroom teacher found the family strange. The sentence broke off in the transcript, so we don't know what happened next.
- **Aunts and relatives who warmed her:**
  - Her 이모 (mother's sister) and 고모 (father's sisters) favored her and gave her more money than her sisters. Her father has six sisters, and she says she resembles them. The youngest 고모 lived in Busan and was a Miss Korea contestant (she "won 미", unclear exactly what placing). She was elegant and learned.
  - The aunts brought special food, including 꼬지전 (skewered pan-fried food) with burdock, beef and carrot, topped with seasoning sauce. She also mentions a version with yellow pickled radish (she called it "독광", probably 다꽝/단무지). The aunts told funny stories and cursed freely, which she says felt like "real life".
  - She says the aunts' 이모/고모 sides never sat together, because her mother looked down on the aunts' schools. This made her anxious to read her mother's moods (눈치) from early on.
  - Cousins on her mother's side apparently respected her mother (she said "고모", but it seems she meant her mother, unclear). Her 이모's children disliked her house for its coldness.
  - Her youngest 고모's husband was happily married to her. She roasted 김 (seaweed) with sesame oil for him. She said "우리 오이 왔나?" (the name or word is unclear) when the narrator visited.
  - Her grandparents (probably paternal, unclear) took her by the hand through the alleys of 삼덕동 to the aunts' houses. They also gave her extra money for Japanese crayons.
  - She once disliked her 고모 because of her mother's words and now regrets not meeting her more. She says she misses both her 이모 and 고모 very much.
- **Her mother's death:** She says her mother did not call her when she was dying, and she did not go. She also did not chase after the inheritance, saying "죽고 나면 돈이 뭐가 필요 있어요". She says she has no regrets. (Handle with care; the 한인회 mention is unclear.)
- **Early writing:**
  - She was in the literary club (문예부) in elementary and middle school and won many prizes, "about 50 years ago".
  - She wrote an essay called 「첫눈」 (First Snow) that won first place and "특선" and was printed in a book (a school or Daegu city publication, unclear). Her 3rd-grade homeroom teacher (name given as "홍나무 선생님", unclear) praised her diary and said she had a gift. At the time she thought of becoming a writer.
  - She says she has no childhood materials left because they were discarded when she moved to America.
  - Her daughter, a Cultural Studies professor in Sydney, urged her to write a novel about her childhood hurts.
  - She says she has not written much because she struggled in America supporting her husband. After about 65, when the children were settled, she felt at ease.
  - A friend's daughter (a Yonsei sociology graduate) once told her she was the smartest woman she knew and had sacrificed a great deal.
- **The first-snow morning (age ~10–11, though 3rd grade would be about 9):**
  - The family lived in a decent hanok, not grand, in Daegu, with 20+ jars on the 장독대 and a well. Her father loved gardening and grew morning glories, balsam, portulaca and four o'clocks.
  - Heavy snow fell overnight, about 2 inches. She saw it first, alone. Her mother warned her it was slippery, and a kitchen helper ("일하는 언니") was inside.
  - Her dog Mary (lived 8 years) ran 3–4 laps around the house and then sat by her foot. She sat on the wet stone by the steps rather than on the porch.
- **Old vs. new Korea:**
  - She loves the old, poorer, "original" Korea and dislikes modern Korea. Apartments feel suffocating, and she says she could not stay even a few days. Before she fell ill she visited once or twice a year. She is not pleased by K-pop or by Korea's development.
  - Daegu had no asphalt even downtown, not even for the Miss Korea parade from Seoul.
  - She says she was never hungry as a child.
- **Maternal grandmother's house in 의성 (Uiseong):**
  - The richest house in the village, with the biggest wooden gate. She went by bus, with a cousin (외사촌 언니) and a male cousin (외사촌 오빠).
  - Meals included a slaughtered piglet and fish. At night the stars were countless. They ate 호박 범벅 (pumpkin mash) with red beans, 울콩 (red beans), black beans and glutinous-rice 수제비 dough, while hearing a wolf story about a pig carried off. She clung to her cousin.
  - In summer they swam in the stream in just underpants. She calls it "a paradise".
  - Asked about her grandmother, she only said "정말로 그렇게 강한 분은 안 계셨죠" (meaning unclear). The interviewer moved on.
- **Smaller points:** She worried the app had stopped recording ("다 했어요가 나오는데"). She said inconsistencies are fine "because this is fiction". She worries her speech is unpolished and said she respects the AI for understanding her.

## Scene notes
- **First snow:** "함박눈이 밤새 내려가지고 아침에 일어나니까" [heavy snow fell all night, and when I woke up...]. About two inches lay on the 장독대, on the tree branches, and on the garden beds where her father grew morning glories, balsam, portulaca and four o'clocks. The 장독대 had 20+ jars of 장 and 고추장, and there was a well.
- **Girl and dog in the snow:** She sat on a wet stone by the steps instead of the dry wooden porch. She dug the snow aside and Mary circled the house 3–4 times, then sat by her foot. Everyone else was inside, and her mother's voice warned her not to slip. The 첫눈 essay was written from this.
- **Her phrasing about the quiet:** "정말로 조용했어요" [It was truly quiet].
- **Aunts' visits:** Food and stories arrive together. 꼬지전 of burdock, beef and carrot, cut long, topped with seasoning sauce. Women gossiping and cursing at the table, "사람 사는 것 같다" [it feels like people are really living]. Without them "딱딱했어요" [it was stiff]. Her mother always on guard and "잘난 체" [showing off].
- **Laver scene:** Her 고모's house, with sesame oil on roasted 김. "아이고, 워낙 많이 무라. 여 많이 재려놨다" [Eat plenty, I've stored lots]. The girl eats only a few sheets out of modesty, and the aunt notices. Her mother's seaweed, even with sesame oil, "그 맛이 안 났어" [didn't taste like that].
- **Alleys of 삼덕동:** Ivy-covered walls, Japanese-era 적산가옥, refined hanok, roses climbing the neighbor's fence. A child walking with her grandparents, since there were no cars. A girl holding crayon money (Japanese-made crayons) she cannot show off, which her older sister later steals.
- **Uiseong:** Bus ride, huge wooden gate.

## Transcript (English)
**Interviewer:** Hello, Mother. I'm glad we can talk again. The story about leaving snacks at the door of your friend Lee Sun-bun's house really stayed with me. Today let's talk a little more about books. While you were reading *Toji*, is there a scene that stays with you most?

**Mom:** Yes, there's Byeoldang Assi [Lady Byeoldang] and her partner, whose name I can't remember. Um... that man, I can't recall his name, but the part where he runs away with Byeoldang Assi and the two of them live very happily together, that part made a strong impression on me. And the grandmother who kept leading that big household even after her husband had all passed away, I liked her too. Anyway, I read *Toji* about ten years ago, and the whole thing was a very beautiful memory for me. Also, when I saw the feasts and the gatherings of so many people and all those servants, it reminded me of my family home in the old days, so it was very interesting, and beautiful, and a bit sad too, and I missed that time. Now that I'm old and the children are all grown, I think that's why I felt that way. And, um, I also liked *Taebaek Sanmaek* a lot, but why do I like this one by Park Kyung-ni? *Taebaek Sanmaek* has some kind of ideology in it, you know. But this one is entirely about family. That's why I took to it, I think.

When I think about it now that I'm old, and I try to be fair, I don't think my family reached that kind of level. Of course we lived a city life and others treated us with deference, and even though my mother was someone educated at a very high-class level, in my heart I don't respect my mother. And my father was the same. But in *Toji* there's a real sense of respect. Hmm. People are all different, of course. But that mother just letting those two go, letting them go for the sake of their happiness, that seems so different from my own mother, my mother in my family home. If I had run off with someone like that, I think my mother would have taken me off to some far-off island and killed me. I think things like that too. So very much, sons, daughters... And I don't know why, but I felt like I was my mother's rival. I loved my mom so much, and I wanted to be held in her arms just once, but not once did I ever get held by my mom. But whenever my imo [maternal aunt] or my oesukmo [wife of my mother's brother] or my gomos [paternal aunts] came, there were three daughters in our house, but they always doted on me most. And when they came I was so, so happy. And when the imo and gomo came, they always gave me more money than my older sister and my younger sister. So, well, I was happy back then. But later, as I got older and think about it now, I was so innocent then. I don't think I thought of my two siblings as rivals. But my older sister and my younger sister (I'm the middle one) always seemed to resent me a little. Because everyone loved me. My mother seemed to feel the same. Back then you couldn't go and confront your mother or anything like that. And my homeroom teacher even went so far as to say this family is strange. **Interviewer:** Ah, so the feasts and the people in the great household in *Toji* reminded you of your family home. You said you were so happy when your imo and gomos came. Could you tell me, whatever comes to mind, what the house looked like on the days they came?

**Mom:** When they came they always brought something delicious, and jeon [Korean pan-fried savory pancakes/fritters] they'd made themselves at home, special jeon. Nowadays if you watch YouTube it's just donggeurangttaeng [round minced-meat patties] and the like, but maybe because they were from educated families, when they brought those things I was so happy. Happy, because I loved their stories so much, their stories. They'd sit and talk, and even swear and carry on, and it was so much fun to watch, because it felt like people actually living. When they weren't there, our house was stiff, just stiff. So my imo's children, my cousins, those cousins on the imo's side, disliked our house. Because there was no jeong [warmth, human affection] there. But my cousins at my maternal grandparents' home, my older female cousins and so on, respected my gomo [aunt; she seems to mean her mother, given what follows]. Because she had graduated from Gyeongbuk Girls' High School. Back in the Japanese colonial era, for a Korean to graduate from there was a big deal. So whenever they had something to discuss on a special occasion, my mom was always the one who taught them and advised them. What I especially remember from when I was little is that when my older cousin got married, mostly into good families, my mother played the biggest role as adviser. So, in a word, in today's language, when it came to putting on airs, she was the best. But the house had no jeong. Always fighting, and my father had no jeong either. I don't know why. I hated that so much. So sometimes I rebelled, in ways I couldn't say out loud.

But my older sister and my younger sister were a bit dull-witted, as I think about it now. I was a little embarrassed. I was the only smart one, and I attended the best schools, so I used to think, how nice it would be if my sisters did well in their studies and my brother did well too. Since I went to top-tier middle and high schools, my friends' older sisters and brothers also went to the very best schools in Daegu. It's not that you have to have a good academic background to be good, but there's a certain level to it, you know? So from childhood I was always aware of that. And over nothing much, hearing that because I was a girl I wasn't worth even the grime on my brother's heel, from childhood I took a lot of that unfairness to heart.
