# Chapter 3: Where Is Clarksville

### 1

By a quarter past one they had run out of doors.

Declan could have drawn the annex from memory by then. There was one long oval of white concrete and mirror floor with a gate area at its center, a restaurant at one end and a bar at the other, the service corridor and its yellow stripe, and the mezzanine glowing like an aquarium with nothing in it. There were four shutters, and each was the same gray as the one before it. If it were a story, he thought, it would be an editor's nightmare. All setting and no exits. A closed room was a sentence that had stopped, and here was the whole paragraph.

They came down the dead escalator in single file and gathered at the gate. Ray did not sit. He stood with his back to the board and the bar on his right and let his eyes go around the group again, face by face, the way he had before. Declan was beginning to understand that it wasn't a gesture so much as a metabolism.

"Pockets," Ray said.

"I'm sorry?" said Anika.

"Everybody empties their pockets. Bags too, on the chairs. I'm not accusing anyone. I want to know what we have in this room, and I want to know it before anyone starts having a good idea."

"That's the most reasonable thing anyone's said to me since the pretzel kiosk," said Leo, "and I want you to know I'm still against it."

"I'll go first," Ray said.

He did, which Declan noted and filed, because it was not what a man with something to hide would do. He laid his possessions along the arm of a chair in a straight line, with no flourish, like a man setting a table. A spare magazine, black and heavy. A pair of handcuffs, folded. Two pairs of gray socks, rolled together. A plastic pill case with the days of the week on the lids, S through S, and the Thursday lid worn pale from thumbs. A paperback with a cracked spine and a woman's silhouette on the cover, its corner dog-eared at a page far enough along that Declan felt, absurdly, a pang of sympathy for the author. Last, with the same lack of ceremony, he drew the pistol, cleared it, and set it on the chair beside the paperback.

"Fourteen-C," Leo murmured. "Told you."

"The socks are for the long-haul legs," said Ray. "Your feet swell."

"I'm not asking."

"You looked like you were going to."

It was the closest thing to a joke Declan had heard out of him, and nobody laughed, because they were all looking at the gun. It was a very small object to have made the room so much smaller.

Hal went next. He put his master ring on a chair, and the dead radio, and a thermos, and a folded newspaper with the crossword half done in a hand so neat it looked typeset. He turned out a wallet with nothing in it that would interest anyone, and Ray flipped it open, looked at the ID, and shut it without a word. Declan, watching, thought the old man's face went very still as the wallet closed, in the way a pond goes still. Then it was only a janitor's face again.

Leo went through his pockets like a magician who has lost interest in the trick. Business cards, a great many, in a silver case, and a loose handful of them on top of that. A pen from a hotel in Tulsa. A flask, dented, which he held up and said, "Medicinal," with great dignity. And last, from the inside pocket of his jacket, a very small stuffed rabbit, gray and worn nearly bald at the ears, no longer than his hand.

Nobody said anything. Leo looked at the rabbit and then at them.

"Don't," he said.

"I wasn't going to—" said Anika.

"You were thinking it. Everybody was thinking it. It's a long story, and I'm not telling it, and it's not evidence." He set the rabbit down on the chair with the delicacy of a man placing a baby in a crib. "I will say that I once closed a merger for a tech company, a very nice, very polite tech company, and there was a page two hundred and twelve. I did not read page two hundred and twelve. I've been on a very strict page-two-hundred-and-twelve diet ever since. That's the whole of my confession."

"What was on page two hundred and twelve?" said Declan.

"A sunset, I assume. A beautiful sunset, and a signature." Leo flapped a hand. "I bill by the hour. I don't read by the hour."

Anika set down her bag and took out, one at a time, a phone, a charging brick, a water bottle, and a laptop with a sticker on its lid that had been scraped half off. The last thing she laid out was a boarding pass, folded into a tight square, soft at the creases from being handled. Ray picked up the laptop and she said, quite evenly, "Don't open it."

"Why not?"

"Because it's work. And because it's mine." A pause. "It isn't dangerous."

"I didn't say it was."

"No," said Anika. "You were thinking it."

Ray looked at her a moment, and then he put it down, and Declan saw him do it, saw the measurement go into the file. He saw something else too. While Ray was looking at the laptop, her thumb had gone to her phone, and she had looked at the black rectangle of its screen the way you look at a window you are waiting to see a light come on in.

Then it was his turn, and he had a problem.

He put out his phone, his wallet, his pen, the flashlight Hal had lent him, a mint tin, a ticket stub from a play he had left at intermission. The mitten was in his left coat pocket, small and blue and slightly damp from his palm, and his hand had gone around it without asking him. He could feel the little loop of yarn where the thumb was. It would be absurd to produce it. He would have to explain, and he didn't have an explanation. He had a tickle at the back of his neck.

"That's it," he said.

"That's the coat pocket, too?"

"It's lint."

Ray's eyes rested on the left pocket of the coat, a thing that cost more than his mother's car had, for exactly as long as it took to be sure Declan had seen them rest there. "All right," he said. Then, mildly, "It's a nice coat."

"It's a *very* nice coat," said Leo.

Declan said nothing. He thought of the two shirts in rotation, and of a hospital vending machine, and he kept his hand in his pocket as though it were warm in there.

### 2

At first Declan thought it was only that he had finally learned to count.

He had gone to sit at the end of the row of gate chairs, as far from the pile of confiscated treasures as he could get without leaving the room, and he was doing what he did when he was frightened, which was to turn the fear into a list. Four shutters. Two stopped escalators. Sixty-four chairs, in rows of eight. Eight times eight. He knew he'd counted sixty-four before, an hour ago, when the room had seemed to him the size of a cathedral, and the number had been sixty-four then, too. There were the four of them and Hal, and five people in a room with sixty-four chairs, and he was counting so he would not have to think about the mitten.

Which is how he saw her.

She was in the last chair of the last row, on the aisle, at the very end of the room's long perspective. She sat with her ankles crossed and her hands in her lap and a small, neat purse on her knee, and she was looking at the departure board with an expression of mild and pleasant patience. A dark blue uniform, pressed. A pale scarf knotted at the throat. A name tag on the left breast that his eyes slid off every time they tried to read it, as though it were a word in a language he'd studied once and lost.

She had not been there. He would have sworn to it. He had walked past that chair six times.

"Excuse me," Declan said, and it came out smaller than he intended.

Ray's head turned. Then everyone's did.

"Hello," the woman said. She had a voice like a good recording of a voice. There was nothing wrong with it, and there was nothing in it either. "I hope I'm not interrupting."

"Who are you?" said Ray. He was already moving, one hand drifting toward the arm of the chair where the pistol lay, and Declan watched him decide not to take it. "How long have you been here?"

"A few minutes." She considered. "I came in a few minutes ago. I'm sorry, I didn't see you. I thought I was the only one."

"You didn't see us," said Leo. "We were *yelling*. A man was holding a gun."

"I didn't see that either." She smiled, faintly, with all the right muscles. "Is that unusual?"

Hal had come to stand beside Declan, and he was looking at the woman with his head slightly tilted, with a courtesy that had a tight thread running through it. "You're with the airline?" he said.

"Yes."

"Which desk?"

"The Dublin desk."

"Where'd you come in from?"

The woman paused. It was a very small pause, and it was not the pause of a person searching for a lie. It was more like a person waiting patiently for a screen to load. "That way," she said, and gestured, vaguely, at the wall.

Anika had come up on Declan's other side. "That's a shutter," she said.

"Is it?" said the woman. She turned her head to look at it. "So it is."

Declan opened his mouth, and above them the departure board flickered.

It was a small thing. A single soft refresh, like a blink. The line that had said **DUBLIN. Gate Z.** since the moment they walked in went dark for one half second, and the letters came back, and they were different.

**CLARKSVILLE. Gate Z.**

For a while, nobody said anything. The dashes in the time column went on being dashes. The new word sat up there in the same clean white font, as ordinary as the old one, as though it had always been the one the building meant.

"No," said Leo. "No, that's not, hang on. That says Clarksville."

"It does," said the woman.

"We're going to *Dublin*."

"Are you?"

"It said Dublin. It said it *a second ago*. We all saw it, you saw it, the man with the gun saw it—"

"It's always said Clarksville," the woman said. She said it without any heat, in the tone you use when you correct a child about the name of a street. "Gate Z. I don't think it's ever said anything else."

Declan looked at Hal, because Hal was the one who worked there. The old man looked at the board for a long moment, and his mouth was a closed line. Then he looked at Declan and made a small, helpless shape with his shoulders.

"Where even is Clarksville?" said Anika, quietly, and it was the first time Declan had heard her ask a question she did not already know the answer to. "Is it in Tennessee? I think it's in Tennessee."

"There's one in Tennessee," said Ray. "There's a Clarksville in Texas. Arkansas." He stopped. "There's a lot of them."

"Which one is this?"

The woman turned her pleasant face from one of them to the next and settled it, at last, on Anika. "Do you really think that matters?" she said.

It was a question, truly asked, with a pale, mild curiosity at the bottom of it, and it sank into the room like a stone into a pool. And Declan, who had spent his whole life collecting the right thing to say, could not think of a single one. Nobody hummed. Nobody said anything about trains. It was, he would think later, the strangest thing about it: how perfectly nobody remarked. They stood in the bright, humming room with a stranger who had not been there and a destination that had never been Dublin, and all five of them were quiet in the same polite way, like people at a funeral for somebody whose name they had been told and forgotten.

### 3

The television over the bar came on at 1:51.

Declan knew because Anika had been holding her phone, and he had seen her mouth shape the time. They had drifted, all of them, toward the gold light of the bar, as people will toward any warm thing in a cold room, and Leo had gone behind it and found the glasses and was pouring something clear into five of them with great solemnity. "For nerves," he was saying. "I'm not saying it'll help. I'm saying it's *traditional*." The woman in the blue uniform had stayed in her chair at the end of the row. She was not watching them. She was watching the board.

The TV was mounted high in the corner, a flat black rectangle nobody had noticed because it had been dead. Then it was not dead. A gray light washed up out of it, the soft, grainy gray of a channel with nothing on it, an old-fashioned hiss that Declan felt in his fillings, though it made no sound. In the center of the gray a small red dot came up and began to pulse. Slowly, like a heartbeat.

"Nobody touch the remote," said Leo.

"There's no remote," said Hal.

A voice came out of the speaker. It was a man's voice, or the shape of one, flat and level, with no accent in it and no emotion, the voice of a thing reading instructions off the back of its own eyelids.

"*Remain indoors,*" it said. "*Remain indoors. Do not go outside before sunrise. Do not go outside before sunrise. Remain*—"

It cut off. The gray stayed. The red dot stayed.

For a long moment no one so much as breathed. Declan became aware of the glass in his hand, and that he had not drunk from it. He thought, with a kind of dim professional detachment, that he would have cut the line. It was too on the nose. A real voice would have given a reason.

And then he glanced at Hal.

Hal was not looking at the screen. He had turned his face half away from it, toward the gleam of the bar, and on that lined, weathered, unremarkable face was an expression Declan had never seen on anyone in real life, only in the very best fiction. It was recognition. But it was the wrong kind. It was the recognition of a man who has heard his own name called in a crowd, a name he'd spent years trying to leave behind him, and who is determined not to turn his head.

It was there for a second. Then it was a janitor's face again, mild and tired. Hal reached up and straightened the mop where it leaned against the bar.

"Sunrise," said Ray. "What's *that* about?"

"I don't know," said Hal.

"You didn't recognize that voice?"

"It's a recording."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Hal agreed. "It isn't."

"Gentlemen," said Anika, "I think we should—"

The floor spoke.

It was not a sound exactly. It came up through the soles of Declan's shoes and the legs of the bar stool, a deep, slow, soft *thud*, like a vast door being pulled shut in a house a hundred miles long. It lasted a few seconds and spread out in all directions. The glass in his hand trembled, once. Then the stillness came back and covered it.

"That was far away," Anika said, very quietly. "That was a long way away."

"How far?" said Leo.

"I don't know. Far."

"I don't *like* far. Far means big."

Everyone was looking up at the ceiling, at the walls, at the TV with its pulsing red dot. Declan looked down.

He did it because of the mop bucket. It sat where Hal had parked it by the end of the bar, a yellow plastic thing on little gray wheels, half full of dishwater, and something in the corner of his eye had been bothering him. The water in the bucket was perfectly still, and it should have been. But as he watched, a ring formed in it. It spread from the center out to the edge in a thin, even circle, as if a single drop had fallen from the ceiling. Then another one followed it, and the surface settled, and the water was still.

Nothing had dripped. He looked up and the ceiling was dry, white and ribbed and perfect.

"Did anybody see—" Declan said, and stopped, because no one was listening to him, and because he did not trust himself to describe what he had seen. He caught Hal's eye instead, and the old man looked at the bucket and then, very slowly, at him, and gave him one small nod. It meant *yes. I saw it. Don't say anything yet.* Declan wondered how he had come to understand a nod like that, from a stranger.

It was Anika who saw the light.

"There," she said. She was pointing at the nearest shutter, the one at the foot of the corridor where the moving walkway had been. "Under the edge. Look."

A thin line of color lay along the floor beneath the steel, where it didn't quite meet the concrete. It was a color Declan couldn't have named: not red, not gold, but a dull, bruised orange, and it was moving. It rose and ebbed as slowly as breath. It pulsed once, twice, and then it faded out like the glow of a coal you were not meant to look at.

"Fire?" said Leo.
