# Chapter 6: Wrong Door

### 1

The service corridor was the only part of the annex that looked like it had ever been meant for work. The walls were bare cinderblock painted the white of old teeth, and the yellow stripe ran down the middle of the floor like the line on a road. Declan walked on the right side of it, because it seemed rude to walk on the stripe.

He had taken a candle. Nobody had asked him to. On the way past the restaurant he had stepped inside, lifted a jar from the nearest table, and carried it out cupped in one hand like a man leaving a church with something he hadn't paid for. The flame stood up straight and small, and the napkin bird on that table sat on its plate watching him go.

"I want it noted," said Leo, behind him, "that the writer has stolen a candle, and that no one has stopped him."

"It's a restaurant. It's a breakfast candle. It's not going to be missed."

"Is that what you tell yourself?"

"It's what I tell the judge."

"Then you'll want a lawyer." Leo produced a card from somewhere and held it out over Declan's shoulder without looking. Declan took it. It said **LEO ADEYEMI-BRANDT, ESQ.**, with a phone number, a second phone number, and, in smaller letters, *Reasonable Rates, Unreasonable Hours.* He put it in his pocket with the mitten.

They had done it Ray's way, which meant they had done it slowly. Ray went first, with the pistol back in his hand and the muzzle low. Hal came behind him with the ring of keys, which Ray had allowed him to take on the grounds that he'd rather have the old man where he could see him than have the keys where he couldn't. Then came Declan and Leo, and Anika at the rear, one thumb on her phone. Declan had expected the woman in the blue uniform to be a problem, to require a decision or a guard. But when Ray had asked her whether she'd like to come with them, she said, "Oh, no, thank you, I'm quite comfortable," and folded her hands, and nobody had known what to do with that. They had left her in the gate area with the board.

It was twenty-six past. The corridor was seventy feet long, and he knew it was seventy because he'd counted the ceiling panels on the first trip. He counted them again now, because it was something to do with his mind.

"Declan," said Anika, from the back. She said it quietly, the way you'd speak to someone on a ledge. "If you're wrong about this."

"Then I'm wrong. I've been wrong in front of larger groups."

"I mean, how will you feel?"

He considered it. It was a strange thing to be asked, and she had asked it in the tone of a person who actually wanted the data. "Relieved," he said. "Mostly. I think I'd be relieved. If it's random, it's just a bad night. If it's a pattern, somebody made it."

"That's what I was afraid you'd say."

He glanced back. She was watching her phone, and the little gray line at the top of its screen, and then she put it in her pocket and walked on with her hands loose at her sides, like a person who had decided to be brave about the next thing and the thing after it.

The fire door was propped open on its bar. Past it was the landing, a concrete box about the size of a walk-in closet, lit by the single caged bulb. At its far end was the fourth shutter, gray and seamless and perfectly clean. Ray stopped in the doorway and held up a hand, and they stopped behind him, in a tight line, breathing.

"Not on the landing," said Ray. "Nobody steps on it. We stand here."

"That's fine," said Declan. "That's actually perfect."

"Why?"

"Because it's the one place in the building where nobody's been in the last ten minutes. If something changes, we'll see it."

He set the candle jar on the floor of the corridor, just on the near side of the sill, and stepped back from it. The flame stood. The shutter stood. Hal, to his left, was looking at the bulb above them with his head tilted, and the old man's lips were moving. Declan thought he might be praying, and then realized he was doing arithmetic.

"Two twenty-nine and forty," said Anika.

"Right."

"Twenty seconds."

"Right."

"You're sure."

"No," said Declan. "I'm not sure of anything. I'd like that on the record too."

"Noted," said Leo, very softly. "Everything's noted. The record is long."

Nobody said anything for a time. Declan became aware of the corridor's silence in a way he hadn't before, because for the first time he was waiting for it to be broken. The hum of the lights was so steady that he had long since stopped hearing it, and now he heard it, a fine high wire of sound running through the building. He heard Ray's breath, which was slow, and Leo's, which was quick. He could not hear Hal's at all.

The flame leaned.

It bent toward the landing, toward the shutter, the way a child's head turns toward a noise in the next room. It did not flicker. It bowed down over the wax with its tip pointed at the gray wall, and held. Declan said, "Now," in a voice so low he barely made the word, and counted. *One. Two.* Behind him he heard Anika's breath catch, and a small, dry click that was Ray thumbing something off. *Three. Four. Five.*

*Six.*

*Seven.*

*Thud.*

It came up through the soles of his feet, as it had five times before. It was longer here, and nearer, and it had a different texture, a grainy rumble at the bottom of it, like something enormous being dragged a short way across a stone floor. The candle jar trembled in the corridor. On the landing, under the shutter, the line of orange light lay down along the concrete and burned there, dull and bruised, and in the glass of the jar he saw the flame stand up again and the little ring of wax shiver like a pond.

A single ring crossed the floor in the corner of the landing, where there was a stain of old damp. It spread to the wall and went out.

Nobody breathed.

Then the orange faded, as it always did, as slowly as a coal goes out, and the shutter was only a shutter.

"Two-thirty," said Anika, in a small voice. "On the dot."

Declan found that he was gripping the doorframe. He let go, one finger at a time, and was surprised to see that his hand was shaking. It was a stupid thing to be shaken by. It had only been a number and a candle.

"Okay," said Leo. He sounded like a man trying to put the lid back on a jar. "So that's, that's very spooky. I'd like to say that I was wrong, for the record. I said horror movie. This is much more *specific* than a horror movie."

"It was a good call," said Ray. He said it without turning his head, still watching the shutter, and the words came out grudging and flat. "It was a good call, Marsh."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. If you can predict it, it's a system. And if it's a system, somebody's running it."

"That's what I said."

"I know what you said." Ray turned at last and looked at him, and for a moment the measuring look was gone and what was behind it was only tired. "I'm saying I didn't want it to be true."

It was the most honest thing Declan had ever heard a man with a gun say, and he didn't know what to do with it. He started to answer, and then Hal said, in a voice that stopped them all:

"That wasn't there before."

### 2

It lay on the landing, a yard in from the shutter. It was a white rectangle of plastic about the size of a credit card, face up on the concrete, sitting at a slight angle like something set down by someone who meant to come back for it.

Declan would have sworn there had been nothing on that floor. He had looked at it for a full minute. He had looked at it the way you look at a page when you're waiting for the first word.

"It wasn't there," said Ray. "Everybody agree? Nobody put that there."

"I've been watching that spot for twenty seconds," said Leo. "A *card* does not just."

"Nobody touch it."

"I'm not touching it. It's very clearly a trap. I'd like to be on record as having said so, before somebody does something heroic."

"It's a key card," said Anika. She had gone down on one knee in the doorway and was leaning forward as far as she could without crossing the sill. Her voice had the thin, happy sound of a person who has found an interesting bug. "Look at the stripe. Mag stripe, and a chip. That's an access badge."

"Access to what?" said Declan.

"I don't know." She tilted her head. "There's printing on it. Hal, can you read that?"

Hal had moved to her shoulder. He bent, and his bifocals flashed in the light of the bulb. "Maintenance," he read. "Annex Nine. All access. It's got a stripe, a little barcode. No name. No picture." He straightened up, and he did not look pleased. "That's not one of ours. Ours say Facilities, and they've got the picture on."

"Maybe it's an old one."

"This building's a month old."

Ray gave it a long, steady look, the one he gave everything. Then he held the pistol out to Hal without looking at him, butt first, the way you'd pass a hot pan, and said, "Hold this. Don't point it at anybody."

Hal took it as though it were an egg. Ray stepped across the sill onto the landing, and Declan noticed that he went flat-footed and slow, like a man crossing ice, and that nothing happened. He crouched and picked up the card with his thumb and forefinger by the edges, and he held it up to the bulb and turned it over. The back was blank white.

"No heat," he said. "It's dry. It's a card."

"There's your way out," said Leo. "A card. Somebody's left us a card. That's, you know, I've been in a lot of buildings, and in my experience when a building gives you a card, it's the *good* news."

"Then where's the reader?"

It was Anika who asked it, and the question hung in the corridor. Declan thought of the doors they'd tried. The gift shop, the kitchen, the closets, all of them opened with a plain brass key, and not one had a reader, a keypad, or a slot. It was an old-fashioned building, he thought, for a new one. It had every modern surface and no modern lock.

"Every door in this annex runs off Hal's keys," said Ray. "So what's the card for?"

"I'd say," said Hal slowly, "it's for a door we haven't found."

"There aren't any more doors."
