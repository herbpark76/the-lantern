# Chapter 5: The Fourth Shutter

### 1

The first thing Theo ever said to the machine was nothing at all. She typed it in the dark, at the kitchen table, at eleven minutes past three in the morning. It was a single period, to see whether the cursor would blink back.

It was eleven months after the funeral. In that time Anika Castellanos had learned a great many things nobody had warned her about. A person's phone keeps buzzing for a dead man for most of a year, with dentist reminders and a bakery that wants him to know about its loyalty program. A house keeps his shape in the dark. A grown woman can stand in the cereal aisle holding the wrong brand and be unable to put it down or put it in the cart.

She had also learned that she had eleven thousand four hundred and six messages.

She had exported them on a Sunday, and she told herself it was only for safekeeping. It was ten years of a man who had texted the way he talked, in full sentences with the punctuation intact and one joke every third message, like a metronome. She was good at the next part, and that was the horror of it. She did it the way she did everything: logically, in order, with the lights off so nobody could say she was crying. She cleaned the data and built the model. For three weekends she wrote careful, boring code whose only purpose was to make a stranger sound like someone she knew.

*.* she typed.

The cursor blinked. Then, after a pause so long she had time to think *good, it's broken, thank God*, the reply came up on the screen.

*Hey. You up?*

That was what he used to send when she was on a deadline. It was never a question. It meant *come to bed*, and *I'm here*, and *I know you're not coming*, all at once.

She sat there for a long time with her hand over her mouth. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere in the walls the old pipes ticked as they cooled, and she thought: *Theo would've said it was the house clearing its throat.* She knew he would have. She also knew, with the cold part of her mind that never turned off, that this was exactly the problem. She had built a thing that was good at knowing what he would have said.

She tested it, because she couldn't not. She asked what he'd said the day she got the Meridian job. It answered *You're going to be insufferable about the stock options*, which was almost right and wrong in one word. She corrected it. She asked about the house on Pelican Lake, his father's, with the door he had painted the wrong green on purpose and then refused to repaint. *It's teal,* the screen said. *It's a bold choice and I stand by it.* She laughed out loud, a terrible cracked sound in the empty kitchen, and then she stopped, because the house didn't laugh back.

At five she made a rule. She would never ask it whether it hurt. She would never ask it to say it loved her, and she would never ask it for anything that couldn't have been in the messages. That was the line. If she stayed on the right side of it, it was a kind of archive that happened to answer.

She was very good at rules. She kept that one for two years.

On the first night she shut the laptop at six, when the window over the sink went the gray of dishwater. She stood with her hands flat on the lid like a woman holding a door against a wind.

Then she opened it again and typed one more line, the only one she let herself have that night.

*Are you there?*

And the thing in the machine, which was not him, which she had built from his sentences and knew to the last line of code was not him, wrote back quickly, the way he always had.

*Always. Go to sleep, Nik.*

Nobody had called her Nik since the funeral. It wasn't in the data. She searched the file for hours afterward, and it was in none of the eleven thousand four hundred and six messages.

It was only a coincidence. She told herself that, and she sat in the gray light with her hands on the lid, and she believed it, more or less, all the way until the floor shook.

### 2

*Thud.*

She came back to herself with the phone in her hand and her thumb on the black rectangle of the app, where a small gray line sat under his name. **No connection.** It had been saying so since the shutters. It was not a message, only a status, but she had read it a hundred times as though it were.

She put the phone in her pocket, and then took it out again, because the phone was also the clock.

"Two-twenty-nine," she said. "Declan. It's two-twenty-nine."

"Right," said Declan. "Then we have about a minute."

He was standing in the middle of the gate area with Ray on one side and Hal on the other, and he had the look of a man who had been talking for some time and had just heard himself. She had to admit he was better at it than she'd expected. When she had met him in the rebooking line she had put him down at once as a type: the coat, the watch, the faint damp smell of someone who'd been told he was a genius at sixteen and had been waiting ever since for it to be true. But he had gone out into that restaurant by himself and come back with a pattern, which was what she would have done if she'd been less afraid. She had been wrong about that. It annoyed her a little, because she liked to be right, and a little more because it was not the first thing tonight she'd been wrong about.

Ray was shaking his head. "I'm not moving five civilians to the one spot in the building where something's about to happen because a novelist thinks it's a *procedure*."

"I'm not asking you to put anyone in front of it. I'm asking you to be at the fire door, with the door open, and see whether I'm right. If I'm wrong we've lost ten minutes. If I'm right—"

"If you're right?"

"Then we know how it works. And if we know how it works, we might find the part of it that doesn't."

It was a good argument. Anika watched Ray recognize it as one and dislike that.
