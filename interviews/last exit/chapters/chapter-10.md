# Chapter 10: Fourteen Minutes

### 1

The number on the board said 3:32, and then it said 3:33, and Declan understood that someone had finally given the building a clock and that the building meant to use it.

Nobody said anything about it. They had stopped commenting on the strange things sometime around the candles, and the habit had settled over the room like politeness. They sat in the circle of chairs with the cardigan over the employee's face. Ray stood with Hal's keys in his pocket, and Hal sat very straight with his hands on his knees, a man waiting to be told he could go.

Declan stood at the bar with his back to the shelf of glasses and his palm against the wall behind it. He had asked to be here for the clock. He was here, in fact, because Hal had said eleven feet, and he wanted to feel what eleven feet of nothing felt like. It felt like plaster. It felt cool. It did not breathe, and he was almost sorry.

"Anika," he said. "What's the log say?"

"Three-thirty-one was the last one." She did not look up from her phone. "West shutter. Thirteen minutes after 3:18."

"Then the next is 3:44."

"Yes."

He watched the board. The candles in the restaurant, a pale gold smear through the open door, stood straight and still. He counted the way he'd counted all night. At 3:41 they leaned.

"That's early," Anika said.

"Three minutes."

"Eleven, then. Not thirteen." She was on her feet. "Everybody stay still."

*Seven seconds. Thud.* It came up through the floor, and it was not the same. It was heavier, and it had a grain in it, like a slab being dragged. The orange under the east shutter lay down along the concrete and burned longer than it ever had before it faded. A ring crossed the bar mat, where a drip of spilled soda sat, and then another ring followed on its heels.

"3:41 and fifty seconds," said Anika. She typed. "Next one."

They waited. It was the worst waiting of the night, because now there was no rule to wait inside. At 3:49 the candles leaned. At 3:49 the thud. Then the orange, at the south end, brighter, nearer, a color you could almost smell.

"Eight," said Leo, in a voice with no joke in it. "It's eight. It went thirteen, eleven, eight. Somebody tell me that's a coincidence. I'll bill them for it later."

"It's accelerating," said Anika.

"Toward what?"

She didn't answer. Declan, who had a trade in answers, found he did not have one either.

### 2

At 3:55 Anika's phone buzzed in her hand.

It was such a small, domestic sound that for a moment nobody understood it. A short vibration, then a second, the noise a phone makes when a message lands. She looked down. Declan saw her face go through something he would have taken a page to describe and then change into nothing at all. It was the progress bar again, held at ninety-nine percent.

"I have a signal," she said.

"That's impossible," said Ray. "Nothing's got a signal. We tried every phone."

"One bar." She turned the screen toward no one. "It's the app. The connection icon. It's green."

"Don't," said Declan, and did not know why he said it.

She was already walking away from them. She went to the far end of the gate area, near the dead windows, and stood with her back to the room and her shoulders drawn up. He found himself following, three steps, four, and then he made himself stop. It was hers. A secret stayed private until the person chose to open it, and he knew better than anyone what it was to be looked at over a clue you had no way of understanding.

But the room was quiet and the building was quiet, and her phone was on speaker. He heard it.

*"Hey. You up?"*

The voice was a man's, warm and a little rough, with the grain in it that a real throat leaves. It was not a good imitation. That was what made his skin crawl. It was a *perfect* one.

Anika said nothing for a long time. Then she said, in a voice so quiet it hardly traveled, "I'm here."

*"You've been gone a while. I was starting to think you'd forgotten me."*

"I didn't."

*"I know. You never do."* A pause, the length of a smile. *"Where are you, Nik?"*

She made a sound. It was small and wrecked and swallowed at once. Declan looked at the floor. He heard Leo shift behind him and Ray start to say something and be stopped, he never saw by whom.

"Terminal Nine," she said. "I don't know where. It's closed. It's, we're stuck."

*"I know,"* the voice said gently. *"It's all right. Listen. Something's going to happen at four. Don't go through the door. Whatever it looks like. Don't go through."*

"What door?"

*"Nik."*

"What door, Theo?"

The green light on the screen blinked. The line crackled, one burst of dry static like a handful of sand. Declan saw her thumb come down hard on the glass.

"Theo."

*No connection,* said the little gray line.

She stood there. He watched her put the phone to her chest with both hands, the way you hold something that has just stopped breathing, and he watched her not cry. It was the most disciplined thing he had ever seen a person do.

Then she turned around, and she was entirely herself.

"That wasn't a recording," she said. "I built him. I know what he can say. He's never said anything he wasn't given. He said *four o'clock*. He said *the door*." She looked at each of them in turn. "Something told it. Something in here knows what's coming, and it let us hear it."

"Or it's bait," said Ray.

"Or it's bait. Yes. That's what I'm telling you. It's one of the two and we don't know which."

### 3

They argued for six minutes. Declan timed it, because it was the only part of the night he could still control.

Ray's position was simple, and it was not stupid. The building had given them a card, then a voice. Both were gifts. Gifts were how you walked a thing into a corner. He wanted the circle of chairs held, the wall left alone, the card kept in his pocket, and everyone in sight of everyone else until the sun came up and the shutters did whatever shutters did. "Sunrise is the one thing it told us," he said. "It's the one rule it gave. We sit on it."

"It's getting faster," said Declan.

"Then we sit faster."

"Ray. At the rate it's going, it doesn't make it to sunrise. Thirteen, eleven, eight. If it keeps going down, we're getting thuds a minute apart by four-thirty. What's at the bottom of that? What happens when the interval hits zero?"

"I don't know."

"Neither do I. But I'd rather find out standing at a door than sitting in a circle."

"You'd rather." Ray's face was quiet. "You've been right once tonight, Marsh. That's one. You made a good call about a candle. It doesn't make you the man to open a wall."

It landed. Declan felt it hit the place under his ribs where Dublin was. He had been right about a candle. He had been right, and he had stood in the corridor with his hand shaking, and for ten minutes the whole room had looked at him the way a room looked at a man with a theory, and he had liked it. It had been the first thing in two years he had liked.

"I'll go with Declan," said Leo.

Everyone turned.

"I'm saying it as a matter of litigation strategy. The guy with the gun wants to sit still while the building counts down. I have never won a case by sitting still while the other side counts down." He swallowed. The rabbit was in his fist. "No offense, Ray."

"I'd stay with Ray," said Hal, quietly. He did not look at Declan. "I'm sorry. He's right to watch me. If I were him I'd want me where I could see me."

It was the saddest sentence Declan had heard all night, and it split the room neatly in two.

Which left Anika.

She had not spoken. She stood at the edge of the circle with the phone against her sternum and her gaze gone inward, doing sums. Declan watched her do it and found that he was holding his breath.

"You're both wrong," she said.

Ray's jaw moved. "Excuse me?"

"It isn't random, and it isn't a countdown to zero." She came into the middle of the carpet. She took the phone from her chest and held it up, with the log on it. "Look at the intervals. Thirteen, thirteen, thirteen. Then eleven, then eight. It didn't start dropping when we found the card. It started when Hal said the word *door*. 3:32. The clock starts at 3:32. The first short one is 3:41. Nine minutes later, and it's shorter every time we get closer to the wall."

"Closer to the wall," Leo said slowly.

"The building's not counting toward sunrise. It's counting toward whatever happens when we find it. We found the *place*, behind the bar, and the pulses got faster. That's not a clock. It's a hot-and-cold game." Her voice was level, but her hands were not. "It wants us there. It's coming faster because we're warm."

Nobody said anything.

"And Theo said four o'clock," she went on. "And don't go through the door. I don't know what that means. But the building has been telling us the truth in the only way it can, which is by doing exactly what it does, on time, every time. So we use that. We stop guessing and we start reading it." She looked at Ray. "You hold the circle. Fine. Somebody has to. But you don't touch the wall without us. And you," to Declan, "don't touch it either."

"You're giving orders," said Ray.

"I'm giving a schedule." She said it without heat, like a woman reading a platform number. "There's a pulse at 3:57. We watch where the orange goes, and we log the interval. Then we vote."

For a long moment the pistol and the keys and the whole slow weight of Ray's certainty stood against her small gray cardigan. Declan had seen leaders made in books. It always happened with a speech.

It happened here with a phone and a column of numbers.

"Fine," said Ray. It came out of him very quietly, like a man setting down something heavy he had been carrying for years. "Log it."

At 3:55 and forty seconds, the candles leaned.

"There," said Anika, and everyone looked where she was looking, and none of them looked at Declan at all, which was how he knew it had changed hands. Behind the bar, behind the shelf of glasses, the plaster wall gave off a thread of orange light the thickness of a pencil line, and for the first time that night, it did not lie down along the floor.

It stood up the wall, and drew a rectangle.
