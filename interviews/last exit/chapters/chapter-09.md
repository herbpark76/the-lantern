# Chapter 9: Hal's Keys

### 1

The building in Dayton had been Hal's, in the sense that a ship is a captain's. His name was not on it. His name was on forty-one pages of drawings in a flat file at a firm that no longer existed, under a title block that read H. KOWALCZYK, P.E., and under that, in his own square hand, *Rev. C.*

He was fifty-one, then. He wore a tie to the site visits and a hard hat over it. He thought a building was a promise made out of concrete: *you will be safe in here.* That was the whole of the job.

The client was called the Foundation, with no surname. The client paid early, which was the first thing that should have worried him. The brief was a four-story records annex with a sub-basement, and it asked for odd things in a polite memo. No windows below grade. A single stair. Shutters in the corridor ceilings, steel, on drop mechanisms, rated to seal a hall in under four seconds. One room on the lowest level with a door that was to be drawn on the plans as a blank wall, with no handle, no hinge, no keyway. *A door is a weakness,* the memo said. *The wall will know who may pass.*

He had asked what it was for. A man in a good gray suit had smiled and said, "Storage."

He built it, because the check cleared and his wife's treatments were not cheap. He poured the sub-basement in March. He signed the final sealing certificate in October, standing in that lowest corridor with the shutters up and the lights humming, a clean white box smelling of cured concrete. At the end of it, set into a stretch of blank gray wall exactly where his drawing said, was a place where nothing was.

He laid his palm against it. It was cool, and it was not quite still. He felt a faint pulse in it, slow, like a sleeper's.

"Does it open?" he'd asked the man in the gray suit.

"For the right person."

"Who's that?"

"Whoever it's keeping."

He had laughed. The man had not.

Eleven months later there were people in that building. He'd read it in a newspaper, in four paragraphs, under a headline about a *training accident*. Nine names. The shutters had come down in the night, the report said, and the fire doors had held, because he had built them to hold. He had built them very well.

He had gone to the site once more. The sub-basement was cordoned. A man stood in front of the tape with a clipboard, and behind him, at the end of the corridor, Hal had seen a door where his drawing said wall. It stood open. There was nothing inside but light.

Then the sirens, the long one with the drop at the end, which the Foundation had specified for the building's alert system, and which he had, God forgive him, approved from a catalog.

### 2

"Hal."

The word came from a long way off. Hal blinked up at the ceiling of Terminal 9 and found it in its usual place. Somebody's hand was under his elbow. It was the writer's, the one with the stolen candle, and the hand was steadier than the face above it.

"Easy," Declan said. "You hit your head."

"I didn't. I just sat down hard."

He sat up. His ears rang with the ghost of the note. The employee lay on the carpet where they had rolled her, eyes open on nothing, the scarf trailing, and the sight of her went through him again, quietly, like a draft under a door. He had touched her shoulder as she fell. He had felt what Declan had felt. There was nothing in there to hold.

Across the circle Ray was on one knee with a palm pressed to his eyes. He had not made a sound since it stopped. He was breathing like a man who has been under water and is determined not to gasp. When he lowered the hand his face was wet and perfectly blank, and for a second Hal thought he knew that look from the mirror.

"Everyone sees?" said Anika. She had her phone again. Her voice was a thread pulled tight. "Say it. Everyone sees?"

"Yes," said Leo. "Colors, and the, the whole lot. I'd like to say I've had worse mornings, but I'd be lying, and I'm sworn not to."

Nobody laughed. Hal got to his feet. The pistol lay a yard away on the carpet, and he looked at it, and found that he did not want it back. He found he'd have been glad if somebody kicked it into the sea.

Ray picked it up.

He did it quickly, almost courteously, and checked it, and put it in his waistband. Then he held out his other hand, palm up, and looked at Hal. "The card."

"You've got the card," said Hal. "It's in your jacket."

"So it is." Ray took it out. He turned it over in the light, the white plastic, the stripe, the barcode, MAINTENANCE ANNEX NINE ALL ACCESS. "Where's the reader?"

"I don't know."

"You've got a master set. You've got keys to the whole annex. You told us that. You've been up and down this building tonight with a ring the size of a hubcap. Where does a card like this go?"

"There's no reader," said Hal. "I'd have shown you."

"There's nothing in the walls. Nothing on a single door." Ray said it slowly. "Hal, a building this new, this much money, and every lock in it is brass. Why is that?"

"I didn't build it."

It came out too fast. He heard it. He heard it land in the circle, and he saw Anika's head come up at it, quick as a bird's.

### 3

"Didn't say you did," said Ray.

"No."

"Said, why brass."

"I'd guess somebody liked brass."

"You'd guess." Ray's eyes had the cold, steady patience of a man laying a second line of evidence beside the first. "You knew the siren. In the first hour. You said fire's three pulses, this was the long one, they quit testing it years back. When the TV came on, you turned your face. I watched you." He took a step. "And a minute ago, with that woman coming at you, you didn't shoot to stop her. You shot like a man who'd been told what she was."

"I shot because she was walking at me."

"Twice. At the chest. As if you knew it wouldn't matter."

"Ray," said Declan.

"Stay out of it, Marsh."

"I'd like to, but you're about to do something stupid, and I'm the only one here who's had practice."

Hal almost smiled at that, and the almost hurt more than a blow. He looked at the faces ringed around him. The lawyer had the rabbit in his hand again, not hiding it, the little gray thing worn to nothing. The woman with the phone had gone the careful, closed color of a person adding a column. And the writer's right hand was deep in his coat as if something in the pocket might otherwise leave.

They were all frightened. That was what he told himself. A frightened room needs a neck to put its hands around, and he had stood in that kind of room before.

"The keys," Ray said. "On the chair."

"They're on the chair."

"The ones on your belt."

Hal looked down. It was true. He had picked them up on the way back down the corridor without a thought, and they hung on his hip, a ring the size of a bread plate. He unclipped them. His fingers knew the weight so well. For thirty years, through three firms and a very long marriage, a good set of keys had meant that the world was a place where doors did what they were told.

He put them in Ray's hand. The ring clinked, once.

"Thank you," Ray said, and it was almost kind. He weighed them. "Now. You're going to tell me what you know."

"I know something," Hal said. "I'm not sure it's what you want."

### 4

They sat in the circle of six chairs, the five of them and the body, because nobody could bring himself to leave her on the carpet alone. Anika had laid her cardigan over the employee's face. It did not look like grief. It looked like manners.

Hal told it plainly. A firm in Dayton. A client with no surname. A sub-basement with shutters in the ceiling and a door drawn as a wall. He did not tell them about the nine names or the newspaper. He said it was twenty years ago, and that he had thought of it perhaps once a week since, which was not a lie.

"A door drawn as a wall," Declan said.

"No keyhole. No handle. No hinge." Hal rubbed his jaw. "I specified the stock. I never saw a pin go in. The wall was the door. It was only that someone, at some point, was meant to be allowed through."

"And the card?"

"It's a key. I'd say it's what the wall wants to see." He lifted his eyes to the long white curve of the ceiling. "A building like that has a fixed plan. The doors don't move. The stair's always in the same quarter. I've been walking this annex all night with the keys in my hand, and the part of my head that's been doing arithmetic since the corridor has been telling me the same thing."

"Which is?" said Anika.

"There's a stretch of wall that's wrong." He turned and pointed down the room, past the gate, past the bar. "The back of the bar. Behind the shelf with the glasses. That wall's got no service door, and a restaurant bar always has one, for the kegs. The kitchen's on the other side. I measured it with my feet on the way through. It's eleven feet short."

"Short of what?"

"Of where it ought to end." He shrugged. "Eleven feet. That's the width of a corridor, and a door at the end of one. That's where I'd put it."

Leo whistled softly. "The bar. The *bar*. Of course it's behind the bar. Everyone knows the good stuff is behind the bar."

"And you'd know," said Ray.

"Excuse me?"

Ray was not looking at Leo. He was looking at Hal, and the pistol was not in his hand, but his hand was near it. "Twenty years ago, you built a door like that. Tonight you point to the place where one would be, and the card in my pocket fits. You see how that sounds."

"It sounds like a man who knows buildings."

"It sounds like a man who knows *this* one."

"Ray," said Anika, quietly. "If he'd wanted us to open it, he wouldn't have had to tell us."

"Unless he needed us to open it." Ray kept his eyes on Hal. "Somebody built this place to hold people. Somebody put a card on that landing right after the thud, with nobody near it. I don't think the building left it there for us. I think it's a thing the building *gives* to the person it's already expecting."

It was clever. It was horribly close to the shape of what Hal feared himself, and he could not say so. He had stood in that corridor in Dayton with his palm on the wall and felt it breathe. He said, "I don't know why it's here. I only know where the door would be."

"That's what I thought you'd say."

Ray stood. He took the key ring out of his left hand, tested its weight, and slid it into the pocket of the windbreaker where the card had been. It was done gently, as a man stores a thing he has decided is his. The room watched it go.

"Nobody opens that wall," he said, "until I say. Nobody walks to the bar alone. And Hal." He paused. "You don't walk anywhere alone. You've got a friend, from now on. Pick one."

"I'll take him," said Declan.

Hal turned. The writer's face was pale and set and rather young. He had the mitten out of his pocket, a small blue wad he had not noticed he'd drawn, and he looked down at it as if somebody had put it in his hand.

"I need to be near the bar anyway," Declan said. "It's where the clock is."

It was not a reason. They both knew it. Hal let it stand.

Behind them, under a gray cardigan, the employee lay where she'd been laid. And above the empty bar the board flickered once, a single soft blink, and the dashes in the time column changed. Where there had been a row of patient lines, a number now sat. *3:32.* It stayed lit, and began, very slowly, to count.
