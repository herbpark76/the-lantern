# Story Bible

# Story Bible

## Working Title
**The Last Flight to Clarksville** (Herb's own title; he calls it cheesy but funny, a nod to The Monkees' "Last Train to Clarksville").

Alternates (suggested, earlier placeholders):
- *Gate Zero*
- *No Departures*
- *Hold Pattern*

## Logline
Five stranded travelers are rebooked into a silent, sealed-off terminal at O'Hare, and they must find a way out before sunrise. The man who insists on leading them is Declan Marsh, an arrogant bestselling novelist who has gone numb to his own life. Over one night he learns that the terminal and the people in it may be a simulation, and that he only starts wanting to live when he is about to lose it.

## Genre and Tone
- Existential mystery thriller and liminal-space horror, written as a **novella of about 30,000 words**, in close third person on Declan. His inner voice is a novelist's: "Well. Here is something that is happening."
- **Darkly funny that turns serious** as the characters crack. Leo carries most of the dry humor.
- The dread comes from the unknown and from distrust inside the group, not from monsters. Creepy things happen, but there is no creature feature. Ghost-story and *Smile*-style creepiness is welcome (a possessed-seeming look, a smile held too long), played quietly.
- **Quiet, wrong, and understated.** Herb prefers silence to spectacle. No voiceless announcements, no obvious winks such as the employee humming the song. Strangeness is plain and nobody comments on it.
- **Slow-burn creepiness.** The odd things go in one at a time, in sequence, and are not piled on at once.
- **Fast-moving.** The action should move quickly, with clues here and there. From about Chapter 4 on, chapters open with a pausing flashback that lets us see a character and then move into fast action. **Chapter 7 is the exception:** it opens straight in dread, on the walk back, and its flashback (Ray's) comes mid-action.
- **The ending is bittersweet first, then warm, with a twist.** It is sad and then hopeful, with a final hook rather than an explanation.

## Comparables
- *Five Characters in Search of an Exit* (Twilight Zone): the direct inspiration.
- Sartre's *No Exit*, *Cube*, and *The Bar*: claustrophobic mystery with existential stakes.
- *Identity* (the John Cusack film): the group leadership shifts. The tough cop takes charge at first, and the main character takes over later. Herb wants to play that trope.
- *Predators*: strangers with common traits and sharp differences, trying to learn why they're all there.
- *Edge of Tomorrow*: an unlikable man is stripped down and becomes someone worth rooting for. The group races and fights toward a glimmer of hope.
- *Sphere*: objects drawn from their memories appear around the characters.
- *The Terminal*: the airport as limbo.
- *Into the Night* (the Belgian Netflix series): a sunrise deadline, with a backstory for each character.
- *Lost*: the flashback structure. The lead gets an early flashback, and the most suspicious characters get theirs later.
- *Better Call Saul* / *Breaking Bad*: Leo's flavor as a shady-adjacent lawyer with an interesting past.
- *The Matrix*: Declan can eventually "see" the script and hack out.
- *Smile* / *Smile 2*: the possessed-seeming look, used for the employee's turn.
- *The Shining* ("Redrum"): the model for the employee's flat, singsong "Biscuit."
- Star Trek TNG episodes in which aliens experiment on the crew.

## Setting
- A **new, nearly empty terminal at O'Hare**, hard to find and reached by rebooking. It is drawn from Herb's real experience of a strange Aer Lingus terminal. The group's flight is bound for **Dublin**, from **Gate Z**. Hal says the wing is a new annex that opens at five for the Dublin run.
- **Middle of the night.** The terminal is supposed to be closed until morning, so there is no staff and no flights. There are humming lights, **escalators stopped mid-step**, and **creepy, total quiet** (no creaks, hum or traffic, and footsteps come back thin). No station announcements. A voice that comes from a TV is allowed.
- **Clock:** chapter one begins roughly forty minutes before boarding. At the end of chapter two it is about **2:00 a.m.** (see open questions on the log times). The board shows a flight departure time that equals **sunrise**, and somebody notices the match later (a phone, for example). At **3:00 a.m., the witching hour (Chapter 7),** the employee turns, the struggle for the gun happens, the sound kills her, and the group realizes something is deliberately being done to them.
- The floors are mirror-bright and freshly mopped, and the arrivals leave no footprints.
- **The shutters come down** and the doors seal, and the windows stop being windows. A shutter coming down at night is alarming to everyone, because it should never happen. A bomb-shelter alarm is part of the sealing. Shutters have no lock, handle, hinge or manufacturer's stamp ("Usually there's a stamp").
- **Outside is dark and unknown.** The danger builds from outside triggers. Already seen: a bar TV waking to dead-channel gray with a red pulsing dot, a flat voice saying "remain indoors... do not go outside before sunrise," a slow deep thud coming through the floor like a huge door closing far away, rings spreading in the mop bucket, an orange pulse under a shutter, and restaurant candle flames leaning.
- **The thirteen-minute pattern (Chapter 5, written):** the thud and orange light recur every thirteen minutes, circling the shutters in turn (log: 1:51 corridor west, 2:04 east windows, 2:17 south by the gift shop). The restaurant candles lean first toward the next point and then, about seven seconds later, come the thud and a ring in a puddle. Declan predicts the fourth point, the landing past the fire door, at about 2:30. The pattern reads as something *checking* whether they are still there. Hal says it will go on until sunrise.
- **The high-pitched sound (Chapter 7):** a dog-whistle-like tone that everyone hears and that incapacitates them. It lands with a gunshot, blinds them like a migraine, and hurts every head. It is recorded sound technology from a recovered craft, and Ray's past connects him to it. It is also what kills the employee.
- Still to come: city lights, explosions, alarms, smoke seeping in, and news of aliens or catastrophe.
- Inside the terminal are the main gate area, a **gift shop** (identical Chicago skyline snow globes tagged WELCOME, neck pillows), a **restaurant** with a spotless kitchen and candle-lit breakfast tables with napkins folded into birds, a **bar glowing gold in the corner**, a service corridor with a yellow floor stripe ending on a fire door, a concrete landing and another shutter, and a glass mezzanine. These are stages for the memory objects and clue props.
- **The Clarksville sign.** The gate display flips from **DUBLIN. Gate Z.** to **"CLARKSVILLE. Gate Z."** just as the airline employee is noticed, and she acts as though it has always said that ("It's always said Clarksville"). The group says they are going to Dublin and asks where Clarksville even is. Nobody hums the song.
- **The Mandela effect (later chapter):** the group clearly remembers being told Dublin, yet Anika's folded boarding pass, and everyone else's, says Clarksville. Nobody can say when it changed. The employee's line turns out to be true.
- **The birthdays:** the six dates are on the Fibonacci sequence (see Clue Ledger).
- **The deadline:** the group believes something terrible happens at sunrise.
- **The service dog** with a birthmark above one eye is in the terminal (see Declan's wound).

## The Experimenters and the Truth
- The experimenters are **future humans, who read as aliens**. They solved everything and have gone numb, and they run a simulation to relearn why life matters by watching people in crisis. It is not an end-of-the-world story.
- **Their aim is feeling.** They push every character toward real emotion (fear, sadness, anything), because all of the characters are numb in their own ways.
- **Declan is the lead architect of the simulation.** Numb himself, he entered his own creation as a test subject. He **wiped his own memory** so he'd be a blank slate and built an **artificial character** to live it. Otherwise he would just have stayed numb. Declan is both architect and test subject. The fictional Declan's backstory is deliberately clichéd for this reason: his memories are implanted, and the flashback should read like the kind of Hallmark-movie memory you've seen before (see the protagonist section).
- **Clue discipline:** the architect twist lives in the bible only. The book gives no hints to it except the line **"He woke up"**, the one sentence of his unwritten novel, which becomes a quiet clue. The clichéd flashback must be played sincerely, with no wink. Quiet signs that the world is a construct (the Fibonacci birthdays, the Clarksville passes, an empty doll body) are fine and are not the architect twist.
- The other people in the terminal are avatars and NPCs. Anika is a character built from a real person, whom Declan seeks at the end. The others carry small pieces of Declan (for example Hal walking away from success, and Anika's silence), a quiet echo that is never fully explained.
- The five were chosen because each is already quietly stuck in life and numb to it.
- The experimenters know every character's memories. Like the experimenters in *Sphere*, they place objects from those memories in the terminal to see which ones make a person feel, and some are frightening rather than tender. They also speak those memories through the puppet (the employee's "Biscuit").
- They also leave **clue props** (a maintenance key card, a boarding pass for a gate that doesn't exist, a luggage tag leading nowhere) that look like ways out and always dead-end. They also push the group to turn on each other. **The key card is unresolved for now**; Herb expects it to matter later, location open.
- **The employee is a puppet.** She is a prop the experimenters use to frighten the group. She is "possessed" at 3:00 a.m., then dies mid-struggle when the sound hits. Her body is empty inside like a doll. Later the body disappears.
- Ray's identity as an air marshal is also built: his badge and gun are props of a role he truly believes. The gun's rounds, the spare magazine and the badge are all fake. Ray does not recognize the badge as his.
- **The reveal comes in steps:** unknown mystery, then the idea that someone or something (aliens, per Ray) is experimenting on them, with the believed end of the world still in the air, then the sunrise truth that none of them are real and the simulation ends with the dawn.
- **The ending (Herb's direction, details open).**
  - Declan believes he is an author but gradually realizes he wrote the script and can break it. He hacks a computer terminal, Matrix-style, and opens the door.
  - The group is free as the sun comes up, and then they fade. Leo and the others disappear first. Declan tells Anika he loves her and she dies. He is alone for a moment with the sunrise, then smiles and fades.
  - He wakes in the pod. The scientists say the experiment was interesting but they still don't understand what happens at the end. He finds he still has all the emotions, and their graph shows it.
  - He wants to find the real Anika. Something she said in the simulation (the house she described, tied to her late husband or partner, the one she talks to through the AI) tells him where to go. Time may pass. He finds her, sees a glimmer of the Anika he knows, and smiles, thinking he might be able to make her feel.
  - Keep the explanation light. It is a hook, not a lecture.

## Themes
- Numbness and the return of feeling: everyone is numb, and the experimenters are poking for any real emotion.
- Wanting to live only when you are about to lose it.
- Control versus acceptance; success as emptiness.
- What is real: identities, memories and tickets that may be built.
- Grief that never closes.

## Protagonist: Declan Marsh, the Writer
- **Name:** Declan Marsh.
- **Who he is:** a novelist whose first novel was a huge hit. He has written nothing in years because, having gotten everything he wanted, life feels boring. (One of his books involved cops, which he uses to press Ray in Chapter 7.)
- **Background (deliberately cliché, since it is an implanted backstory):** a poor white kid from Youngstown raised by a single mom, a waitress, in a broken-home childhood. He rises to fame and becomes numb because he is so well off. His success sits on top of deep insecurity.
- **Flaw:** arrogance as armor, and materialism. He name-drops, pulls rank (agent, publicist, a festival man in Dublin) and is a bit of a dick. He starts out hard to like, and Herb wants the reader to come around to him by the end.
- **Want:** to take charge and refuse the situation.
- **Need:** to feel again and to care about something. He must let go of control and accept what is true.
- **Leadership arc:** Ray leads first. In chapter two Declan refuses to sit and wait ("No"), then comes along with the flashlight and notices what others miss (the bucket rings, the leaning flames). He takes charge in the pattern chapter (Chapter 5), when he spots the thirteen-minute circuit Ray misses. At the midpoint Ray's authority cracks, and Declan presses him on the badge and the gun.
- **Voice:** smart and quiet, with dry, sarcastic lines. Under pressure he becomes panicky and unfocused, and he sells reasonable explanations as a way of managing his own fear ("a very considerate quarry"). He narrates his own life in a novelist's phrasing, such as "A closed room was a sentence that had stopped."
- **His wound:** the one thing he truly loved was his childhood beagle, **Biscuit**. His family could not afford to feed it, and his mom drove it away while he watched. He never saw it again, never knew what became of it, and never let himself have another dog. The grief carries guilt and never closes.
  - **The birthmark:** the beagle has a small birthmark above one eye, on top of its head. The terminal's service dog has the same birthmark, only older.
- **His flashback (Chapter 4, the Hallmark memory):**
  - He is nine, on the fire escape in Youngstown.
  - His mom comes home from a double shift in her waitress apron and counts tips that are still not enough.
  - She falls asleep mid-count, he covers her with his coat, and he promises "someday."
  - The beagle nuzzles an empty bowl and his mom whispers "I'm so sorry." She drives the dog away and he watches it go, wearing the blue mitten.
  - The flashback returns to the present with the mitten in his pocket as the floor thuds.
- **Props:** a small blue child's mitten found on a stopped escalator, which he pocketed. It is the mitten from his memory of the beagle leaving, so it is a memory object the experimenters placed. In the text it is unexplained until his flashback.
- **Chapter 7:** the employee says "Biscuit" to him in a flat singsong. He hides his reaction, tells no one, and keeps the mitten in his pocket. He presses Ray on the badge and gun using what he learned researching cops.
- **His ending:** he feels sad that his life is over. In this one night he truly feels alive, and he only wanted to live once he knew he would lose it. He fights, hacks the door, they get out and nothing changes, and he finally lets go as the sun rises.

# Structure and chapter plan

## Format and length
A novella of about 30,000 words, in twelve chapters. This is Herb's stated target.

## Acts
**Act I (Chapters 1–3, about 7,700 words).** Five strangers are rebooked into a silent new terminal at O'Hare. The gate sign flips to Clarksville and a strange employee appears. The doors and shutters seal, Ray takes charge, and Declan refuses to wait. The outside warnings begin, and the TV announces that no one should go outside before sunrise. The deadline is set.

**Act II (Chapters 4–9, about 16,000 words).** Declan's flashback deepens the mystery, and he takes the lead by spotting the thirteen-minute pattern. The search for exits runs into dead-end clue props. At the midpoint (Chapter 7) the employee turns at 3:00 a.m. and dies, the blanks reveal Ray's badge and gun as fake, and the group thinks it is an alien experiment. Distrust grows, and Leo's and Hal's flashbacks arrive. The body is opened and found empty, then vanishes. Act II ends at the low point, when the Clarksville passes and Hal's deduction reveal that none of them are real and that the night ends with dawn.

**Act III (Chapters 10–12, about 6,100 words).** Declan accepts the truth and realizes he wrote the script. He hacks a terminal and opens the door. The sun rises, the others fade, Anika dies after his "I love you," and he fades last. He wakes in the pod with the feelings intact, then finds the real Anika as a final hook.

## Major plot points
- **Inciting incident (Chapter 1):** the rebooking into the strange terminal, the Clarksville sign flip, and the employee noticed.
- **First plot point, end of Act I (Chapter 3):** the TV warning ("do not go outside before sunrise") and the first thud. The group now has a deadline.
- **Midpoint (Chapter 7):** the employee dies at 3:00, the blanks and fake badge are exposed, and Ray floats the alien idea.
- **Low point / second plot point (Chapter 9):** the Clarksville passes, the missing body and Hal's flashback leave the group knowing none of them are real.
- **Climax (Chapter 11):** Declan opens the door, the sun rises, and the others fade.
- **Resolution (Chapter 12):** the pod, the graph, and the hunt for the real Anika.

# Chapter 7: The Witching Hour

At 2:41 they started back toward the gate, and nobody said what they were thinking, which was that they were walking toward something.

They went in a loose line down the service corridor, Hal in front with the flashlight, then Anika, then Leo, then Declan. Ray took the rear. The yellow stripe ran along the floor like a line somebody had drawn to keep them honest. Behind them the fire door stood shut on the landing and the key card and the second shutter. Ray had put the card in his jacket pocket without a word. He'd done it the way a man pockets a receipt for something he doesn't plan to return.

Hal carried the pistol in his right hand, pointed at the floor, the way a man holds a hot pan he's been told to carry to the next room. Ray had handed it over on the landing, grip first, and walked away from it. Nobody had liked what that said about either man.

"Seven minutes," Anika said. She didn't look at her phone. She had it in her palm, screen up, and the log was open on it like a missal. "2:43."

"Thank you, Anika," Leo said. "Some of us were hoping to be surprised."

The thud came at 2:43. It came up through the mirror floor into the soles of Declan's shoes, slow and deep, a very large door closing in a very large house. The candles in the dark restaurant they passed were already leaning, and then they were not. Declan counted, because counting was something to do with his mind. Seven seconds. On the floor of the kitchen doorway the water in a dropped cup shivered in a single ring that spread to the edge and stopped.

"Right on time," Leo said quietly. "I'd like to speak to whoever is managing this person's calendar."

"Don't," Ray said from the back. It wasn't angry. It was only a word he had to put down somewhere.

Declan walked with his right hand in his coat pocket. The mitten was there, small and blue and slightly damp, as it had been since the escalator. He had not taken it out in an hour. He had not thought about it in an hour, which, for a man who had been working all his life at not thinking about things, was a considerable feat. He noticed that the not-thinking had begun to feel like holding a door closed against a wind.

*Well,* he thought. *Here is something that is happening.*

The sentence didn't help. It had stopped helping around the time the candles leaned.

### 2

The gate area was as they had left it, which was the first wrong thing. Everything was lit, and everything was in place. The rows of chairs faced the board, and the board said CLARKSVILLE. Gate Z in soft white letters, as though it had always said that and was a little tired of being asked. The six chairs they had pulled into a circle stood in the middle of the carpet with Hal's ring of keys lying on the seat of one, where Ray had told him to leave them.

The employee's chair at the end of the row was empty.

Declan stopped, and the line stopped behind him in a small accordion of bodies. The chair was an ordinary chair. A crease in the vinyl seat showed where someone had sat, and the crease was already unmaking itself.

"She was right there," Leo said. "I would like it noted that I did not blame her for anything. I was nice to her. I offered her a card."

"She said she'd keep it," Anika said.

"She did say she'd keep it."

"Spread out a little," Ray said, and then, because the words had come out of him before the rule did, he corrected himself. "Not near the walls. Center. Eyes up."

They looked, because he'd said to. It was Hal who saw her first. He didn't say anything. He only lifted the flashlight, though the gate lights were bright, and held the beam like a man pointing with his whole arm.

She was standing at the south shutter.

The south shutter was the steel one beyond the gift shop, the one that had pulsed orange at 2:17 while the candles leaned and a puddle rang. She stood a few inches from it, close enough that her scarf would have brushed the metal if it had hung a little farther forward. Her hands hung at her sides. Her head was tilted to the left, slightly, as if she'd been told something in a language she was almost fluent in, and she was waiting for the rest of the sentence.

She was perfectly still. Declan watched her for the length of time it took him to be certain of that, and in that interval she did not blink, shift her weight, or breathe in a way he could see.

"What," Leo said, very softly, "is wrong with that woman."

"Nothing's wrong with her," said Ray. "She's near a wall." His voice cracked on *wall*. He had given them the rule at 1:50. Nobody touches a shutter. Nobody goes near the walls. The rule had been Ray's, and it was the only thing in the room that belonged to anyone, and it was the first thing she had broken.

"Ma'am," Ray called. "Ma'am, I need you to step away from that."

She didn't move.

"She's listening to it," Anika said.

Nobody asked her how she knew. It was true the way a diagnosis is true, in the instant it is said. The woman in the blue uniform and the pale scarf was listening to the steel as if the steel were saying something, and what it said was long, and she did not want to miss any of it.

Anika's phone made a small sound as she typed. *2:51 — employee at S shutter. Not moving. Head tilted L.* Her thumb was perfectly steady. Declan found that he could hate her for it, a little, and admire her for it in the same breath. It was the sort of thing he used to put in books.

"Somebody go get her," Leo said. "Somebody who isn't me."

"Nobody goes anywhere," Ray said. "She comes to us."

"That's not a plan, Ray, that's a hostage negotiation."

They stood there. The lights hummed in the way the lights did, which was not at all. Declan became aware that his own breathing was the loudest thing in the building and that the thin echo of it coming back from the glass was a half-second late.

At 2:56 the thud came again. The employee did not flinch. Nothing around her changed, except that the candles in the restaurant, on the far side of the dark, leaned toward her like flowers toward a window. Seven seconds later the ring ran across the water in the cup of the nearest vending machine, outward and outward, until the edge took it.

### 3

At three o'clock exactly, Anika said, "Three," and the woman at the shutter began to hum.

It wasn't a tune. It was a single held note, low in the throat, like the sound a refrigerator makes when it thinks nobody's listening. It lasted about as long as a breath. Then she said, in a voice that was perfectly pleasant, in the flat singsong of a child counting stairs:

"Biscuit."

She had not turned. She was talking to the steel, and the steel was giving her an answer. But the word went across forty feet of carpet and found Declan in the middle of the circle, and it fit him the way a key fits a lock it's been cut for.

"Biscuit," she said again. "Biscuit. Biscuit."

"Who's Biscuit?" said Leo.

Declan's hand closed around the mitten in his pocket. It was damp and small, and the wool had a little give to it, like a hand. He said nothing. He gave his face the expression he used to give interviewers when they asked what his mother thought of the book. It was a pleasant, expensive blank. He felt Anika's eyes turn toward him and then, with great tact or great calculation, turn away.

"It's nothing," he said. "It's a word. She's a malfunctioning—she's tired."

"Is that the word you want to go with," said Anika, "tired."

"I want to go with something."

The woman turned around.

It was done slowly, the whole body at once, the way a figure on a music box turns. Her face came around last. She was smiling. It was the smile she'd worn when she sat at the end of the row at the beginning of the night, a polite airline smile, and it was exactly the same, and that was the trouble with it. She held it past the point where it should have gone, past the point where a smile lets go of a face. Declan began to count and gave up at nine. Her eyes had stopped blinking. They were open and wet and attentive and perfectly empty, like glass over a photograph of someone's eyes.

"Do you really think that?" she said to him, pleasantly.

"Ray," Hal said. He said it the way a man says the name of a ship when he's seen the iceberg. "Ray, I don't like it."

"Stay where you are, ma'am." Ray's hand had gone to his jacket and found nothing. It had been going to his jacket all night, and each time it found less. "Stay right there."

She took a step.

She did not hurry. That was the thing Declan would remember after, when he tried to explain it to himself in the rational tone he used for explaining things. There was no run and no lunge in the usual sense of the word. She walked toward them across the carpet with her hands open and her scarf perfectly arranged, one foot and then the other, a woman crossing a lobby to greet an old friend, and she kept smiling, and she said:

"We're all gonna die."

She said it the way you'd say *good evening*.

"Hal," Ray said. "Hal, put it on her."

Hal had already raised the pistol. He held it in both hands, with the stiff, correct grip of a man who had been shown how once, at a safety seminar, a long time ago, and had never imagined he'd need it. The barrel shook in a small circle. "Ma'am," he said. "Ma'am, I'm going to ask you to stop. I don't want to do this."

"Hal," she said. She had read it off his shirt, or she hadn't. The shirt said JUST HAL. "Hal, you've done this before."

The old man's face did something Declan couldn't read.

"Stop," he said. "Stop. I will."

She didn't stop. She was twenty feet away, and then fifteen. The smile had not moved. Declan thought of a puppet being walked across a stage by a hand that had lost interest in the story and only wanted to see the thing finished.

Hal fired.

The sound was enormous in the silence, the first real noise the building had made all night. The first shot went into her chest, or at her chest, and she did not stagger and did not slow. No hole opened in the blue uniform. She did not so much as flinch.

He fired again. "Oh, God," he said, "oh, God, oh, God." The second one cracked, and there was the smell of burnt powder, and nothing happened to her at all. She was ten feet away. She was five.

Ray was moving. Declan saw him go, a big man reaching for a gun that was in someone else's hand, and he saw Leo fall back against a chair, and he saw Anika's mouth open on a word. The woman reached Hal and put one hand, gently, on the pistol, and one on his shoulder, as if to steady him. Her face was very close to his, still smiling, her eyes wide open.

Then the sound came.

### 4

It wasn't a noise. A noise has a direction. This had none. It was a note so high it passed through the bone behind the eyes and began there, a thin bright wire pulled through the center of his head and drawn tighter. It filled everything. The gunshots, if they were still echoing, were gone. The lights were gone. Declan went to his knees without deciding to. His hands were over his ears, and that was a stupid thing to do, because the sound was not in the air. It was inside, and the pain was the white blind pain of the worst migraine of his life, a flash that took the whole room and turned it to glare. He couldn't see. He couldn't have said whether his eyes were open. Somewhere far away someone was screaming, and he thought it might be him.

Beside him, or inside him, Ray Dunleavy was somewhere else.

### 5

He was in a hangar with the doors rolled shut and the fluorescents humming. He was thirty-four years old, in the Air Force, and he was proud of what he did, though he was not permitted to say what that was.

The thing on the table wasn't large. They called it the unit, and it was the size of a lunchbox, with a grille at one end the color of a mouth. It had been built by the lab from a recording made off a recovered craft. Nobody in the lab had ever said *craft* out loud. They said *the source*. The source had made a sound. The sound had been studied, cut, reduced, and amplified, and then someone, God knew who, had worked out how to play it again.

He remembered Pruitt, the tech they'd put in the room for the third trial. Pruitt had been a good kid with a wife and a lacrosse stick in the trunk of his Civic. The unit had sounded once, for under a second, and Pruitt had gone to his knees with his hands over his eyes and stayed there eleven minutes, blind, saying *I can't see, I can't see*, in the reasonable voice of a man waiting for a bus. Then he could see again, and he'd cried, and no one could say why.

Ray had signed the memo. *No lasting effects.* He'd signed it because the colonel wanted it signed and because he believed, the way a man believes in rain, that nothing he could do would stop the thing from going on.

He remembered the Saturday. It was a swim meet, in a town forty minutes out. Maggie was ten and wore a cap that she'd decorated with a marker, and she sat in the back seat swinging her heels against the upholstery. He had the unit in a gym bag on the seat beside her. He'd taken it home to finish a calibration report because he was behind, and a man who is behind will take a lot of things home. He told himself it was off. He had checked that it was off.

"What's in the bag, Dad?"

"Work."

"Boring work or cool work?"

"Boring," he said, and she laughed at the way he said it, and the unit's grille, in the dark of the bag, found a signal he had not known was in the car.

It sounded once.

He remembered it as light, not sound. The windshield went white. He remembered the wheel pulling in his hands, a weight that was no longer his, and his daughter's voice from the back saying *Dad? Dad?* in the small polite tone of a child who has noticed something is wrong with the grown-ups. Then the guardrail, and the long quiet after it.

She had been dead before the ambulance came. The unit in the bag had gone on running, quietly, in the wreck, until somebody found it and turned it off.

There had been a hearing, and then there hadn't, because the records were sealed. There had been a year of not sleeping, and then his wife had gone to her sister's and then hadn't come back. And then there had been five years of flying in a windbreaker with a badge, a man who rode the planes the way other men take a walk, and who asked for the only job in the world where no one would ever ask him to listen to anything.

The pitch went on in his head, thin and exact. It was the same pitch. He'd have known it in his sleep.

### 6

When it stopped, the stopping was almost worse. The silence rang. Declan was on his hands and knees on the carpet with spots drifting across a gate area that had gone back to being a room, and the first thing he registered was that he was alive, and the second was that his mouth tasted of copper.

The lights came back up to what they had been. Leo was on the floor beside a chair, one hand clamped over his eyes. Anika was on her knees with her phone fallen next to her and her fingers pressed to her temples. Ray was on his side, curled, with his big hands over his face.

Hal was down.

The old man lay on his back on the carpet, the pistol a yard from his open hand, and the employee was on top of him. She had fallen across him the way a coat falls, in a fold, and one of her arms lay out along the carpet with the palm turned up. Her scarf had come loose. She was perfectly still.

"Hal?" Declan said. His voice sounded like something dragged across gravel. "Hal."

"I'm all right," said Hal, from under her. "I'm—I think I'm all right. Get her off me. Please."

Declan crawled over. Leo, whom he would never have credited with the nerve, came from the other side, and between them they rolled her off the old man and onto the carpet. She was light. That was the thing his hands told him before his mind could be told anything. She weighed almost nothing, less than a coat, less than a child.

She lay on her back with her eyes open.
