# Story Bible

# Story Bible

## Working Title
**The Last Flight to Clarksville** (Herb's own title; he calls it cheesy but funny, a nod to The Monkees' "Last Train to Clarksville").

Alternates (suggested, earlier placeholders):
- *Gate Zero*
- *No Departures*
- *Hold Pattern*

## Logline
Five stranded travelers are rebooked into a silent, sealed-off terminal at O'Hare, and they must find a way out before sunrise. The man who insists on leading them is Declan Marsh, an arrogant bestselling novelist who has gone numb to his own life. Over one night he learns that the terminal and the people in it may be a simulation, and that he only starts wanting to live when he is about to lose it.

## Genre and Tone
- Existential mystery thriller and liminal-space horror, written as a **novella of about 25,000–30,000 words**, in close third person on Declan. His inner voice is a novelist's: "Well. Here is something that is happening."
- **Darkly funny that turns serious** as the characters crack. Leo carries most of the dry humor.
- The dread comes from the unknown and from distrust inside the group, not from monsters. Creepy things happen, but there is no creature feature.
- **Quiet, wrong, and understated.** Herb prefers silence to spectacle. No voiceless announcements, no obvious winks such as the employee humming the song. Strangeness is plain and nobody comments on it.
- **Fast-moving.** The action should move quickly, with clues here and there. From about Chapter 4 on, chapters open with a pausing flashback that lets us see a character and then move into fast action.
- **The ending is bittersweet first, then warm, with a twist.** It is sad and then hopeful, with a final hook rather than an explanation.

## Comparables
- *Five Characters in Search of an Exit* (Twilight Zone): the direct inspiration.
- Sartre's *No Exit*, *Cube*, and *The Bar*: claustrophobic mystery with existential stakes.
- *Identity* (the John Cusack film): the group leadership shifts. The tough cop takes charge at first, and the main character takes over later. Herb wants to play that trope.
- *Predators*: strangers with common traits and sharp differences, trying to learn why they're all there.
- *Edge of Tomorrow*: an unlikable man is stripped down and becomes someone worth rooting for. The group races and fights toward a glimmer of hope.
- *Sphere*: objects drawn from their memories appear around the characters.
- *The Terminal*: the airport as limbo.
- *Into the Night* (the Belgian Netflix series): a sunrise deadline, with a backstory for each character.
- *Lost*: the flashback structure. The lead gets an early flashback, and the most suspicious characters get theirs later.
- *Better Call Saul* / *Breaking Bad*: Leo's flavor as a shady-adjacent lawyer with an interesting past.
- *The Matrix*: Declan can eventually "see" the script and hack out.
- Star Trek TNG episodes in which aliens experiment on the crew.

## Setting
- A **new, nearly empty terminal at O'Hare**, hard to find and reached by rebooking. It is drawn from Herb's real experience of a strange Aer Lingus terminal. The group's flight is bound for **Dublin**, from **Gate Z**. Hal says the wing is a new annex that opens at five for the Dublin run.
- **Middle of the night.** The terminal is supposed to be closed until morning, so there is no staff and no flights. There are humming lights, **escalators stopped mid-step**, and **creepy, total quiet** (no creaks, hum or traffic, and footsteps come back thin). No station announcements. A voice that comes from a TV is allowed.
- **Clock:** chapter one begins roughly forty minutes before boarding. At the end of chapter two it is about **2:00 a.m.** The board shows a flight departure time that equals **sunrise**, and somebody notices the match later (a phone, for example). At **3:00 a.m., the witching hour,** something happens that makes the group realize something is wrong (what it is is undecided).
- The floors are mirror-bright and freshly mopped, and the arrivals leave no footprints.
- **The shutters come down** and the doors seal, and the windows stop being windows. A shutter coming down at night is alarming to everyone, because it should never happen. A bomb-shelter alarm is part of the sealing. Shutters have no lock, handle, hinge or manufacturer's stamp ("Usually there's a stamp").
- **Outside is dark and unknown.** The danger builds from outside triggers. Already seen: a bar TV waking to dead-channel gray with a red pulsing dot, a flat voice saying "remain indoors... do not go outside before sunrise," a slow deep thud coming through the floor like a huge door closing far away, rings spreading in the mop bucket, an orange pulse under a shutter, and restaurant candle flames leaning.
- **The thirteen-minute pattern (Chapter 5, written):** the thud and orange light recur every thirteen minutes, circling the shutters in turn (log: 1:51 corridor west, 2:04 east windows, 2:17 south by the gift shop). The restaurant candles lean first toward the next point and then, about seven seconds later, come the thud and a ring in a puddle. Declan predicts the fourth point, the landing past the fire door, at about 2:30. The pattern reads as something *checking* whether they are still there. Hal says it will go on until sunrise.
- Still to come: city lights, explosions, alarms, smoke seeping in, and news of aliens or catastrophe.
- Inside the terminal are the main gate area, a **gift shop** (identical Chicago skyline snow globes tagged WELCOME, neck pillows), a **restaurant** with a spotless kitchen and candle-lit breakfast tables with napkins folded into birds, a **bar glowing gold in the corner**, a service corridor with a yellow floor stripe ending on a fire door, a concrete landing and another shutter, and a glass mezzanine. These are stages for the memory objects and clue props.
- **The Clarksville sign.** The gate display flips from **DUBLIN. Gate Z.** to **"CLARKSVILLE. Gate Z."** just as the airline employee is noticed, and she acts as though it has always said that ("It's always said Clarksville"). The group says they are going to Dublin and asks where Clarksville even is. Nobody hums the song.
- **The deadline:** the group believes something terrible happens at sunrise.

## The Experimenters and the Truth
- The experimenters are **future humans, who read as aliens**. They solved everything and have gone numb, and they run a simulation to relearn why life matters by watching people in crisis. It is not an end-of-the-world story.
- **Declan is the lead architect of the simulation.** Numb himself, he entered his own creation as a test subject. He **wiped his own memory** so he'd be a blank slate and built an **artificial character** to live it. Otherwise he would just have stayed numb. Declan is both architect and test subject. The fictional Declan's backstory is deliberately clichéd for this reason: his memories are implanted, and the flashback should read like the kind of Hallmark-movie memory you've seen before (see the protagonist section).
- **Clue discipline:** the architect twist lives in the bible only. The book gives no hints to it except the line **"He woke up"**, the one sentence of his unwritten novel, which becomes a quiet clue. The clichéd flashback must be played sincerely, with no wink.
- The other people in the terminal are avatars of real people. Anika is a character built from a real person, whom Declan seeks at the end.
- The five were chosen because each is already quietly stuck in life.
- The experimenters know every character's memories. Like the experimenters in *Sphere*, they place objects from those memories in the terminal to see which ones make a person feel, and some are frightening rather than tender.
- They also leave **clue props** (a maintenance key card, a boarding pass for a gate that doesn't exist, a luggage tag leading nowhere) that look like ways out and always dead-end. They also push the group to turn on each other.
- **The reveal comes in three steps:** unknown mystery, then the believed end of the world, then the sunrise truth that none of them are real and the simulation ends with the dawn.
- **The ending (Herb's direction, details open).**
  - Declan believes he is an author but gradually realizes he wrote the script and can break it. He hacks a computer terminal, Matrix-style, and opens the door.
  - The group is free as the sun comes up, and then they fade. Leo and the others disappear first. Declan tells Anika he loves her and she dies. He is alone for a moment with the sunrise, then smiles and fades.
  - He wakes in the pod. The scientists say the experiment was interesting but they still don't understand what happens at the end. He finds he still has all the emotions, and their graph shows it.
  - He wants to find the real Anika. Something she said in the simulation (the house she described, tied to her late husband or partner, the one she talks to through the AI) tells him where to go. Time may pass. He finds her, sees a glimmer of the Anika he knows, and smiles, thinking he might be able to make her feel.
  - Keep the explanation light. It is a hook, not a lecture.

## Protagonist: Declan Marsh, the Writer
- **Name:** Declan Marsh.
- **Who he is:** a novelist whose first novel was a huge hit. He has written nothing in years because, having gotten everything he wanted, life feels boring.
- **Background (deliberately cliché, since it is an implanted backstory):** a poor white kid from Youngstown raised by a single mom, a waitress, in a broken-home childhood. He rises to fame and becomes numb because he is so well off. His success sits on top of deep insecurity.
- **Flaw:** arrogance as armor, and materialism. He name-drops, pulls rank (agent, publicist, a festival man in Dublin) and is a bit of a dick. He starts out hard to like, and Herb wants the reader to come around to him by the end.
- **Want:** to take charge and refuse the situation.
- **Need:** to feel again and to care about something. He must let go of control and accept what is true.
- **Leadership arc:** Ray leads first. In chapter two Declan refuses to sit and wait ("No"), then comes along with the flashlight and notices what others miss (the bucket rings, the leaning flames). He takes charge in the pattern chapter (Chapter 5), when he spots the thirteen-minute circuit Ray misses.
- **Voice:** smart and quiet, with dry, sarcastic lines. Under pressure he becomes panicky and unfocused, and he sells reasonable explanations as a way of managing his own fear ("a very considerate quarry"). He narrates his own life in a novelist's phrasing, such as "A closed room was a sentence that had stopped."
- **His wound (revised, session 4):** the one thing he truly loved was his childhood beagle. His family could not afford to feed it, and his mom drove it away while he watched. He never saw it again, never knew what became of it, and never let himself have another dog. The grief carries guilt and never closes.
  - **The birthmark:** the beagle has a small birthmark above one eye, on top of its head. The terminal's service dog has the same birthmark, only older.
- **His flashback (Chapter 4, the Hallmark memory):**
  - He is nine, on the fire escape in Youngstown.
  - His mom comes home from a double shift in her waitress apron and counts tips that are still not enough.
  - She falls asleep mid-count, he covers her with his coat, and he promises "someday."
  - The beagle nuzzles an empty bowl and his mom whispers "I'm so sorry." She drives the dog away and he watches it go, wearing the blue mitten.
  - The flashback returns to the present with the mitten in his pocket as the floor thuds.
- **Props:** a small blue child's mitten found on a stopped escalator, which he pocketed. It is the mitten from his memory of the beagle leaving, so it is a memory object the experimenters placed. In the text it is unexplained until his flashback.
- **His ending:** he feels sad that his life is over. In this one night he truly feels alive, and he only wanted to live once he knew he would lose it. He fights, hacks the door, they get out and nothing changes, and he finally lets go as the sun rises.

# Structure and chapter plan

## Format and length
A novella of about **30,000 words** (the bible's range is 25,000–30,000; this plan uses the top of it). Chapters 1–3 are written and come to about 10,000 words. Chapter titles for 1–3 below are working placeholders.

## Acts
**Act I (Chapters 1–3, ~10,000 words).** Five strangers are rebooked into a hidden, nearly empty terminal at O'Hare. The sign flips to Clarksville, an airline employee turns up unseen, the shutters come down, and the TV tells them not to go out before sunrise. Ray takes charge, and Declan refuses to sit and wait. Act I ends when the group accepts that they are sealed in with a deadline.

**Act II (Chapters 4–9, ~14,500 words).** Declan's flashback opens the confrontation and then he takes charge by spotting the thirteen-minute pattern. Act II ends at the low point, when the group breaks apart and hope seems gone.

**Act III (Chapters 10–12, ~5,500 words).** Ray's late flashback releases the last red herring. The sunrise truth that none of them are real surfaces, and Declan realizes he wrote the script. He hacks the terminal, opens the door, and the group is free as the sun comes up. Then they fade. Declan wakes in the pod, finds he still feels, and goes looking for the real Anika.

# Chapter 4: What the Candles Knew

### 1

He was nine, and the fire escape was the only place in the apartment that belonged to him.

It hung off the back of the second floor on Delason Street in Youngstown, three flights of rust over a yard full of other people's broken things. In January the iron burned through his jeans. He sat there anyway, in his one good coat, with his knees up and his chin on them, watching the alley for the headlights of the Buick. His mother's shift at the Golden Skillet ended at eleven. On the double days it ended whenever the last drunk left.

The Buick came at ten past one. He heard the door and the keys and her shoes on the stairs, and then the kitchen light came on behind him, yellow and small.

"Declan Marsh," she said from the window. "It is *freezing*."

"I was waiting."

"I know you were, baby. Come in."

She was still in the apron, the brown one with the coffee stain over the pocket. She sat down at the kitchen table without taking her coat off and emptied the pocket in front of her: ones, mostly, soft as cloth, and a drift of quarters, and one five folded into a square so small it must have lived in her shoe. She smoothed each bill flat with the side of her hand and stacked them by denomination. Her lips moved. He had watched her count so many times that he could do it with her, silently, from the doorway. Thirty-one. Forty-six. Fifty-two and a quarter.

She started over. It came out the same.

Under the table, something nosed at a metal bowl and pushed it an inch across the linoleum. Biscuit was a beagle, the color of toast, with ears too big for the rest of him. Above his left eye was a small tan mark like a thumbprint, a smudge God had left there while the rest was still wet. He licked the empty bowl all the way round, and then he looked up at the two of them with the whole hopeful weight of his face.

His mother put her hand over her mouth.

"I'm so sorry," she said. She was not talking to Declan. She leaned down, and the dog came to her at once and put that stupid, perfect head in her lap. "I'm so sorry, sweet boy. I'm so sorry."

"I can eat less," said Declan. "I don't mind. I can have half of mine and give him—"

"No."

"I *can*—"

"Declan." She said it very gently. It was the gentleness that frightened him. "No."

He stood in the doorway, and nobody said anything, and the refrigerator shuddered and was still.

She went back to the money because there was nothing else to go back to. A minute later he saw her stop in the middle of a stack, her hand flat on the bills, and then her head went down onto her folded arm. She was asleep before the next breath, just like that, the way a person falls who has been standing for sixteen hours. A single quarter rolled off the edge of the table and spun on the floor and lay down.

He took off his coat. It was too small for her, and he laid it across her shoulders anyway, tucking it up around her neck the way she did for him. Her hair smelled of fryer oil and cigarettes that weren't hers. In her sleep she was frowning.

"Someday," he told her. He whispered it, in case it was a thing that only counted if no one was listening. "Someday I'm going to get you a house with a yellow kitchen. And you won't ever count again. I promise. I *promise*."

The dog came and lay across his feet, and they stayed that way until it was light.

In the morning she was up before he was, dressed, with her face washed and her eyes red. She didn't say anything about it. She found the leash on its hook and clipped it on, and Biscuit, who thought leashes meant everything good, danced in a circle and sneezed.

"Mrs. Alvarez at the shelter on Market says they find them homes," his mother said. "Good ones. Families with yards. He'll eat twice a day, Declan. He'll get—" She stopped. "He'll be better off."

"You said he was *ours*."

"I know I did."

He did not cry. That was the thing he would remember, later, in a hundred interviews he never told it in. He walked down the three flights beside them with his jaw locked, and he stood at the curb while she opened the back door of the Buick and the dog jumped in, tail going, thrilled by the adventure of it. He had on the blue mittens she'd knitted him at Christmas, one of them darned at the thumb. As the car pulled out, Biscuit scrambled up onto the back seat and planted his front paws on the rear window, with his ears blown flat, and looked at Declan through the glass, puzzled.

Declan raised his hand. It was the only thing he could think to do.

He kept it up as the Buick reached the corner. He kept it up as the brake lights came on and the blinker went, left, and the car turned, and there was nothing at the end of the street but a street. He stood there with his arm in the air until it ached and the mitten was dark with the damp, and he did not put it down.

Then the floor moved under his feet.

### 2

*Thud.*

He was standing in the gate area of Terminal Whatever-It-Was, in a good wool coat, with his right hand deep in the pocket and the other mitten's small damp weight closed in his fist. The lights hummed. The floor shone like a lake. The thud came up through his shoes, slow and enormous, the sound of a door the size of a county shutting in the dark, and along the bottom of the corridor shutter a thin line of orange lay down on the concrete, brightened, and went out.

"Not fire," said Ray.

He had crossed the floor to the shutter before anyone else had finished the word. He did not touch the steel this time. He stood a careful arm's length from it and let the orange come and go along the floor, and Declan watched the light climb the toes of the man's shoes and fall away again. Then Ray stepped back, which was worse than if he had stepped forward.

"No heat off it," he said. "No smoke. Somebody tell me what lights up like that and doesn't burn."

"Neon," said Declan. His voice sounded to him like someone else's, on a radio. He took his hand out of his pocket.

Everyone looked at him.

"Or a, I don't know. A work light. A tarmac crew. They have these big sodium floods for the night shifts, and if one of them is cycling behind a barrier, you'd get a pulse. And the thud." He heard himself speeding up, and could not find the brake. "Blasting. There's a quarry out past the cargo yards, I read about it on the shuttle. They do pressure tests on the new structures at night, when there's no air traffic. It's a very ordinary thing. It sounds enormous and it's a very ordinary thing."

"At two in the morning," said Anika.

"They don't like to disturb the passengers."

"There aren't any passengers."

"Well, then it's a very *considerate* thing."

Leo, from behind the bar, raised the bottle in a small salute. "I'd like to put that on the record," he said. "The writer would like it known that the apocalypse is a considerate quarry."

"I didn't say apocalypse."

"You said it with your *face*."

He had. Declan knew it, and he put his hand back in his coat pocket, where the mitten was. This was what he did. Under pressure he turned into a man selling reasonable explanations door to door, and the person he was selling them to was himself. He had done it to a hospital administrator once, and to a lender, and to a woman in a kitchen who had asked him whether things were going to be all right. He was extremely good at it. It had never once worked.

"The recording said sunrise," said Ray. "That's not a quarry. That's a time. Someone has a schedule." He turned and looked down the long row of chairs at the end of the room, where the woman in the blue uniform had not moved. "Ma'am."

"Yes?"

"You heard the broadcast."

"I did."

"What do you make of it?"

She seemed to consider this honestly, her head tilting a degree to one side, the pale scarf lifting and settling. "Now we know when we're leaving," she said pleasantly.

It was not a threat. That was what made Declan's skin move on his arms. She said it the way a person reads out a platform number, glad to have helped, and then she went back to watching the board, where a single word sat in clean white letters and nobody could make it say anything else.

"Leaving *what*?" said Ray. "Leaving where? Who's leaving?"

"All of us, I should think." She smiled. "Do you really think it could be otherwise?"

Ray took a step toward her and stopped. Declan saw him do it, saw him run the same measurement he ran on everyone and get, for the first time, nothing back. He looked like a man putting his hand into a drawer for a tool he knew he had put there. After a moment he turned away, and his voice, when he used it, was perfectly level.

"New rules," he said. "Nobody goes near a wall. Nobody touches a shutter. We pull in toward the middle, three-sixty visibility, and we hold. If anything comes through, it comes through into a room with a gun in it, and it's mine." He looked at Hal. "And I want your keys back on the chair where I can see them."

"They're on the chair," said Hal.

"I know where they are. I want to *see* them."

Hal looked at the ring and then at Ray, and he said nothing, and in the pause Declan understood that the room had acquired a new pair of eyes with a new place to rest. He did not like what it did to the old man's shoulders.

"I'm going to write things down," said Anika, to no one. She had her phone in her palm.

"Write what?"

"What happens, and when." She said it flatly, almost shyly. "Everyone's going to remember it differently. In an hour we won't agree on what we saw."

"There's no signal," said Ray.

"It's a clock," said Anika. "It's the only thing in my phone that works."

### 3

It took her twelve minutes to make the first note and a little over a quarter of an hour to make the second, and by then Declan had left the group.

He hadn't meant it as a protest. He had stood in the circle of chairs where Ray had pulled them, with the gun on the seat by Ray's knee and the lights humming overhead, and he had felt the same cold fog he'd felt on the plane out of Frankfurt two years ago, the one that came down when a room had too many people in it who wanted something from him. And his hand would not leave the pocket. So he went where the quiet was. He took the flashlight, which he didn't need, because the lights were all on, and he walked across the mirror floor, with the strange sense of being walked beside, and let himself into the restaurant.

It was the nicest room in the annex. It was the only place that looked like someone had wanted a person to be happy in it. The tables were set for a breakfast that had not happened yet, with white napkins folded in the shapes of small birds, and each had a candle in a jar. There were perhaps thirty of them, and all of them were lit.

He stood in the doorway for a while, doing what he did, which was to look at things in order to avoid looking at himself. The candles burned straight and small and yellow. The kitchen's steel door was open, and past it the unlit burners stared back like a row of closed eyes. In the glass of the wall, which was not a wall but a shutter pretending, his reflection waited with its hands in its pockets.

He thought: *I used to be a person who noticed things.* It had been his whole stock in trade, at the start. A woman on a bus with a bruise she'd covered. The way a boy at a funeral held his father's hand too tightly. When you were nine on a fire escape you noticed everything, because noticing was the only thing in the building that was free. He'd been paid for it since. He'd been paid so well that eventually he had stopped doing it, and when he stopped, he found he'd stopped doing everything else too.

*Notice,* he told himself, sourly, in the voice of an editor. *Go on. For once.*

The flames leaned.

It happened all at once, thirty of them, like a field of wheat in a gust. They didn't flicker. They tipped, each one bending the same way, away from the kitchen and toward the long wall at the end of the room, and held there with their tips pointing like compass needles, like a congregation turning to look at something. There was no air. He held up a wet finger, the way you do, and the room was as still as the inside of a jar.

He had the strangest feeling that it was a held breath, and that the thing doing the holding was very large.

*Thud.*

It came up through the floor six or seven seconds after the flames had begun to lean. He felt it in his knees. The napkin birds shivered on their plates. In the corner of his eye, in the dark gap between a table and a banquette, a drift of water crossed a puddle where no water had been, in one thin ring, and the flames let go of their lean and stood upright again, as though nothing had asked them anything.

Through the open door and across the concourse he saw the others turn toward the far shutter, where a thread of orange was lying down along the concrete, and he heard Anika say, in her very level voice, "Two-oh-four."

He was almost sure of it by then. He didn't trust it, and so he did what he'd done with a hundred plots that wouldn't hold together: he waited, and he turned his back on the evidence so it wouldn't know he was looking.

The second time he stayed in the restaurant and watched the candles.

It took thirteen minutes. He knew because he counted, silently, in the dull part of his head that kept lists, and when he got to nine hundred and some he stopped counting seconds and began counting flames. They leaned. Seven seconds. The thud. A ring in a puddle by the banquette. And this time the flames had pointed not at the long wall but at the end wall, the short one, a quarter-turn to the left of where they'd pointed before.

He walked out of the restaurant with his heart going and the flashlight in his fist like a club. He did not remember crossing the floor.

"Two-seventeen," said Anika.

"Which shutter?"

"The far end, by the gift shop." She looked up, and her face changed. "What?"

"Show me the log."

She held out the phone. In the notes app, in the plain gray font of a person who does not decorate things, she had typed three lines.

*1:51 TV. Thud (long). Orange: corridor shutter, W end.*
*2:04 Thud. Orange: east windows.*
*2:17 Thud. Orange: S end, by gift shop.*

"Thirteen minutes," Declan said. "Thirteen, thirteen. You see it? Every one is thirteen."

"I saw that," said Anika. "I wasn't sure it meant anything."

"And they're not random. West, east, south." He was drawing it in the air with the flashlight, an idiot, a man conducting an orchestra. "The corridor. The windows. The gift shop end. It's going around."

"Around what?"

"Us. The building. It's walking the edge of the room." He turned to the shutters in their places, one after another, as if they might step forward and offer a statement. "I've been in the restaurant. The candles lean first, toward wherever it's going to be. Then you get about seven seconds, then the thud, then the ring in the water. Then the light under the door. Every time. It's, there's a *procedure*. It doesn't want to get in. It's *checking*."

"Checking what?" said Ray, who had come up on his other side, quietly, which was a thing the man did.

"Whether we're still here."

Ray's face did not move. "That's a nice theory."

"It's four shutters," said Declan. "One at each end. West, east, south. We've had three. The fourth is the landing, at the end of the yellow stripe, the one past the fire door. It's thirteen minutes from two-seventeen."
