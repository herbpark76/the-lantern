# Story Bible

# Story Bible

## Working Title
**The Last Flight to Clarksville** (Herb's own title; he calls it cheesy but funny, a nod to The Monkees' "Last Train to Clarksville").

Alternates (suggested, earlier placeholders):
- *Gate Zero*
- *No Departures*
- *Hold Pattern*

## Logline
Five stranded travelers are rebooked into a silent, sealed-off terminal at O'Hare, and they must find a way out before sunrise. The man who insists on leading them is Declan Marsh, an arrogant bestselling novelist who has gone numb to his own life. Over one night he learns that the terminal and the people in it may be a simulation, and that he only starts wanting to live when he is about to lose it.

## Genre and Tone
- Existential mystery thriller and liminal-space horror, written as a **novella of about 25,000–30,000 words**, in close third person on Declan. His inner voice is a novelist's: "Well. Here is something that is happening."
- **Darkly funny that turns serious** as the characters crack. Leo carries most of the dry humor.
- The dread comes from the unknown and from distrust inside the group, not from monsters. Creepy things happen, but there is no creature feature.
- **Quiet, wrong, and understated.** Herb prefers silence to spectacle. No voiceless announcements, no obvious winks such as the employee humming the song. Strangeness is plain and nobody comments on it.
- **Fast-moving.** The action should move quickly, with clues here and there. It alternates with pausing flashback chapters that let us see the characters.
- **The ending is bittersweet first, then warm, with a twist.** It is sad and then hopeful, with a final hook rather than an explanation.

## Comparables
- *Five Characters in Search of an Exit* (Twilight Zone): the direct inspiration.
- Sartre's *No Exit*, *Cube*, and *The Bar*: claustrophobic mystery with existential stakes.
- *Identity* (the John Cusack film): the group leadership shifts. The tough cop takes charge at first, and the main character takes over later. Herb wants to play that trope.
- *Predators*: strangers with common traits and sharp differences, trying to learn why they're all there.
- *Edge of Tomorrow*: an unlikable man is stripped down and becomes someone worth rooting for. The group races and fights toward a glimmer of hope.
- *Sphere*: objects drawn from their memories appear around the characters.
- *The Terminal*: the airport as limbo.
- *Into the Night* (the Belgian Netflix series): a sunrise deadline, with a backstory for each character.
- *Lost*: the flashback structure. The lead gets an early flashback, and the most suspicious characters get theirs later.
- *Better Call Saul* / *Breaking Bad*: Leo's flavor as a shady-adjacent lawyer with an interesting past.
- *The Matrix*: Declan can eventually "see" the script and hack out.
- Star Trek TNG episodes in which aliens experiment on the crew.

## Setting
- A **new, nearly empty terminal at O'Hare**, hard to find and reached by rebooking. It is drawn from Herb's real experience of a strange Aer Lingus terminal. The group's flight is bound for **Dublin**, from **Gate Z**. Hal says the wing is a new annex that opens at five for the Dublin run.
- **Middle of the night.** The terminal is supposed to be closed until morning, so there is no staff and no flights. There are humming lights, **escalators stopped mid-step**, and **creepy, total quiet** (no creaks, hum or traffic, and footsteps come back thin). No station announcements. A voice that comes from a TV is allowed.
- **Clock:** chapter one begins roughly forty minutes before boarding. At the end of chapter two it is about **2:00 a.m.** The board shows a flight departure time that equals **sunrise**, and somebody notices the match later (a phone, for example). At **3:00 a.m., the witching hour,** something happens that makes the group realize something is wrong (what it is is undecided).
- The floors are mirror-bright and freshly mopped, and the arrivals leave no footprints.
- **The shutters come down** and the doors seal, and the windows stop being windows. A shutter coming down at night is alarming to everyone, because it should never happen. A bomb-shelter alarm is part of the sealing. Shutters have no lock, handle, hinge or manufacturer's stamp ("Usually there's a stamp").
- **Outside is dark and unknown.** The danger builds from outside triggers. Already seen in chapter two: a bar TV waking to dead-channel gray with a red pulsing dot, a flat voice saying "remain indoors... do not go outside before sunrise," a slow deep thud coming through the floor like a huge door closing far away, rings spreading in the mop bucket, an orange pulse under a shutter, and restaurant candle flames leaning. Still to come: city lights, explosions, alarms, smoke seeping in, and news of aliens or catastrophe.
- Inside the terminal are the main gate area, a **gift shop** (identical Chicago skyline snow globes tagged WELCOME, neck pillows), a **restaurant** with a spotless kitchen, a **bar glowing gold in the corner**, a service corridor with a yellow floor stripe ending on a fire door, a concrete landing and another shutter, and a glass mezzanine. These are stages for the memory objects and clue props.
- **The Clarksville sign.** The gate display flips from **DUBLIN. Gate Z.** to **"CLARKSVILLE. Gate Z."** just as the airline employee is noticed, and she acts as though it has always said that ("It's always said Clarksville"). The group says they are going to Dublin and asks where Clarksville even is. Nobody hums the song.
- **The deadline:** the group believes something terrible happens at sunrise.

## The Experimenters and the Truth
- The experimenters are **future humans, who read as aliens**. They solved everything and have gone numb, and they run a simulation to relearn why life matters by watching people in crisis. It is not an end-of-the-world story.
- **Declan is the lead architect of the simulation (new, session 3).** Numb himself, he entered his own creation as a test subject. He **wiped his own memory** so he'd be a blank slate and built an **artificial character** to live it. Otherwise he would just have stayed numb. Declan is both architect and test subject. The fictional Declan's backstory is deliberately clichéd for this reason (see the protagonist section).
- **Clue discipline:** the architect twist lives in the bible only. The book gives no hints to it except the line **"He woke up"**, the one sentence of his unwritten novel, which becomes a quiet clue.
- The other people in the terminal are avatars of real people. Anika is a character built from a real person, whom Declan seeks at the end.
- The five were chosen because each is already quietly stuck in life.
- The experimenters know every character's memories. Like the experimenters in *Sphere*, they place objects from those memories in the terminal to see which ones make a person feel, and some are frightening rather than tender.
- They also leave **clue props** (a maintenance key card, a boarding pass for a gate that doesn't exist, a luggage tag leading nowhere) that look like ways out and always dead-end. They also push the group to turn on each other.
- **The reveal comes in three steps:** unknown mystery, then the believed end of the world, then the sunrise truth that none of them are real and the simulation ends with the dawn.
- **The ending (Herb's direction, details open).**
  - Declan believes he is an author but gradually realizes he wrote the script and can break it. He hacks a computer terminal, Matrix-style, and opens the door.
  - The group is free as the sun comes up, and then they fade. Leo and the others disappear first. Declan tells Anika he loves her and she dies. He is alone for a moment with the sunrise, then smiles and fades.
  - He wakes in the pod. The scientists say the experiment was interesting but they still don't understand what happens at the end. He finds he still has all the emotions, and their graph shows it.
  - He wants to find the real Anika. Something she said in the simulation (the house she described, tied to her late husband or partner, the one she talks to through the AI) tells him where to go. Time may pass. He finds her, sees a glimmer of the Anika he knows, and smiles, thinking he might be able to make her feel.
  - Keep the explanation light. It is a hook, not a lecture.

## Protagonist: Declan Marsh, the Writer
- **Name:** Declan Marsh.
- **Who he is:** a novelist whose first novel was a huge hit. He has written nothing in years because, having gotten everything he wanted, life feels boring.
- **Background (deliberately cliché, since it is a backstory he invented):** a poor white kid raised by a single mom, with a broken-home childhood. He rises to fame and becomes numb because he is so well off. His success sits on top of deep insecurity.
- **Flaw:** arrogance as armor, and materialism. He name-drops, pulls rank (agent, publicist, a festival man in Dublin) and is a bit of a dick. He starts out hard to like, and Herb wants the reader to come around to him by the end.
- **Want:** to take charge and refuse the situation.
- **Need:** to feel again and to care about something. He must let go of control and accept what is true.
- **Leadership arc:** Ray leads first. In chapter two Declan refuses to sit and wait ("No"), then comes along with the flashlight and notices what others miss (the bucket rings, the leaning flames). He takes charge in chapter four, when he spots the pattern Ray misses.
- **Voice:** smart and quiet, with dry, sarcastic lines. Under pressure he becomes panicky and unfocused, and he sells reasonable explanations as a way of managing his own fear. He narrates his own life in a novelist's phrasing, such as "A closed room was a sentence that had stopped."
- **His wound:** the one thing he truly loved was his childhood beagle. When it died he never let himself have another dog.
- **Props:** he pocketed a small blue child's mitten found on a stopped escalator. It is unexplained and kept as a possible memory object.
- **His ending:** he feels sad that his life is over. In this one night he truly feels alive, and he only wanted to live once he knew he would lose it. He fights, hacks the door, they get out and nothing changes, and he finally lets go as the sun rises.

# Chapter 2: Early

The man who came around the end of the bar was somewhere past sixty, narrow through the shoulders, in gray coveralls faded to the color of dishwater. He had a mop in one hand and a yellow bucket on wheels in the other. He steered the bucket with his knee, with the ease of someone who had done it ten thousand times. A name patch over his pocket said HAL in red thread. His face was lined and mild and tired in a deep, settled way. Declan found himself looking at it a half second too long, with the faint, irritating tickle of a word on the tip of his tongue.

"Sorry," Hal said. "Didn't mean to startle anybody. I figured you were lost. They'll do that to you with the signs."

"Early," said Leo. "You said early. For what?"

"The morning flights." Hal glanced at the shutters and then, with no particular alarm, at the pale ceiling. "Terminal's closed overnight. Last departure was out of Concourse B around nine. They open this wing at five for the Dublin run, and I'm supposed to have it clean before then. So you folks are early. By about four hours."

"We were told there was a flight," Anika said. She had her phone out and was frowning at it. Twelve fifty-four, with no bars. "Tonight. We were forty minutes from boarding."

"Huh," said Hal. He considered this with real courtesy, as though she had mentioned an unusual weather pattern. "Well. Somebody must've made a mistake."

"Somebody sealed us in," said Declan. "Did you see that? The shutters. Does that happen every night?"

"Not that I know of. I don't usually work this wing. I'm over in Three, normally. They put me on here tonight because it's new and nobody's broken it in yet." He shrugged. "It's a new building. Things malfunction. I bet there's a sensor in the ceiling somewhere that thinks it's on fire."

"There was a *siren*," said Leo.

"New sensors are very enthusiastic." Hal frowned a little then, the first sign of any friction in him. "Though that wasn't a fire tone, now that I think of it. Fire's three pulses. This was the long one, with the drop at the end. They quit testing that one years back."

"How would you know that?" said a voice, and the man in the windbreaker stepped between them and the bar.

It was such a reasonable sentence, Hal's, spoken in such a reasonable voice, that for a moment the whole room had wanted to believe it. Declan had felt it like a draft. Now the draft went out of the room.

"I used to work around that sort of thing," Hal said.

"What sort of thing?"

"Buildings." He smiled slightly. "Sir, I'd put the mop down, but you haven't asked me to."

"Put down the mop."

Hal leaned it against the bar with great care, as if it might be offended. "Sure."

"Name?"

"Hal."

"Hal what?"

"Just Hal, on the shift sheet." The old man's eyes were steady. "What's yours?"

The man in the windbreaker took a leather folder from inside his jacket and opened it. He held it at the exact height of a man who had done it in a hundred airports and been ignored in ninety of them. "Ray Dunleavy. Federal Air Marshal Service. I'm working the Dublin flight."

"I *knew* it," said Leo, delighted, and then, hearing the room, less so. "I mean, I didn't know it. I noticed the arm thing. You keep touching your jacket."

"Habit," said Ray.

"So you're the guy," Leo went on, unable to stop, "who's supposed to be sitting in 14C in a fleece, pretending to read a thriller."

"It's a paperback," Ray said, without any expression at all. "Mostly I look at the other passengers."

He closed the folder. Declan noticed that he did not put it back in the same pocket. The gun was in there, then. He felt the relief of it, and a sharper, less comfortable feeling underneath that he couldn't name. Someone in the room now had permission to be afraid for them, and it wasn't him.

"All right," Ray said. "Here's how this goes. Nobody panics, and nobody wanders off. We don't know what that was, so until we do, we treat it as a security event. Hal, what keys have you got?"

Hal lifted a ring from his belt the size of a bread plate. "Master set for the whole annex. They gave it to me at the desk."

"What do you open with it?"

"Everything," said Hal. "That's what a master set is."

"Then we're going to find out." Ray looked around the group, once, one face at a time, the way you'd check a row of locks. "Sweep. Pairs, always. Hal, you're with me. Ms.—"

"Anika."

"Anika, you take the lawyer."

"I have a name," said Leo. "It's Leo. It's on my card. Would you like a card?"

"No."

"I'll leave a few on the bar."

"And you," Ray said, and turned to Declan, "stay put in the main concourse, in sight of the board, and keep an eye on—"

"No," said Declan.

He heard it come out, and he heard it keep coming. "I'm sorry, but no. I'm not going to sit in a chair while someone who may or may not be a federal agent decides which of us is useful. I have people who are expecting me in Dublin. I have an agent, a publicist, a man from a *festival*—"

"Great," said Ray. "Then somebody's already looking for you." He did not raise his voice, and it hurt far more than if he had. "Come with us, then. Carry the flashlight."

Hal, who had said nothing, produced a small flashlight from his coverall and held it out to Declan, handle first, with a look on his face that wasn't a smile and wasn't far from one.

Declan took it. It was heavy, and warm from the old man's pocket. He had nothing to say about it at all.

---

They found out quickly that Hal's keys opened everything. They opened the gift shop, where the racks held a hundred identical snow globes of the Chicago skyline, each with a little tag that said **WELCOME**, and behind them a stockroom of unopened neck pillows. They opened the restaurant's kitchen, which was steel and spotless, with a row of unlit burners like a bank of closed eyes. They opened a utility closet and a family restroom and a service corridor whose floor was painted with a long yellow stripe that ran away from them and disappeared around a corner. Every lock turned with a clean, oiled click, as though each had been tested that morning.

None of them led out.

The service corridor ended in a fire door, and the fire door opened onto a concrete landing lit by a single caged bulb and bounded on the far side by another sheet of gray steel. Ray set his palm against it. He crouched to the seam, exactly as he had before. Hal tried six keys in a smooth, patient sequence, as if the shutter had a lock and the lock was merely being stubborn. It did not have a lock. It had no keyhole, no handle, no hinge, no panel, no manufacturer's stamp.

"Funny," Hal said. "Usually there's a stamp."

They tried the other end of the annex, and the other end had a shutter too. They tried the mezzanine, which they reached by a pair of escalators that were not running. They were frozen in the middle of their motion, the steel steps pulled out into a staircase of gentle, regular teeth. Declan climbed beside Ray, one hand on a rail so clean that it squeaked. Halfway up he stopped. A pale shape hung motionless on the edge of the nearest step, and he leaned closer to look. It was a child's mitten, small and blue, lying on the metal as if it had been dropped a moment before the power went. In a building that had never opened.

He said nothing. He wasn't sure why. He pocketed the mitten the way you'd pick up a coin on the street, and he felt Ray's eyes on his back, and felt that the look had measured something and filed it again.

The mezzanine was a glass gallery, shuttered. Below it the main concourse stretched away, as white and still as a photograph of itself. Hal's radio, clipped at his shoulder, produced a sound like a long breath over sand when he keyed it. "Maintenance, this is Hal in Nine," he said, and let go, and waited. The static went on. He tried it twice more, with the same mild courtesy, as though the radio might simply be taking a moment to compose a reply.

"What's the plan if nobody answers?" said Declan.

"Someone'll answer," Hal said. "Or they won't, and we'll wait till five. Either way it's the same."

"That's very Zen."

"It's very union."

"You're awfully calm for a man who's locked in a box."

Hal looked at him. His eyes were the kind that had been a different color once, before life had washed them. "I've been in worse rooms," he said.
