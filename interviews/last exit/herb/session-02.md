# Story Bible

## Working Title
**The Last Flight to Clarksville** (Herb's own title; he calls it cheesy but funny, a nod to The Monkees' "Last Train to Clarksville").

Alternates (suggested, earlier placeholders):
- *Gate Zero*
- *No Departures*
- *Hold Pattern*

## Logline
Five stranded travelers are rebooked into a silent, sealed-off terminal at O'Hare, and they must find a way out before sunrise. The man who insists on leading them is Declan Marsh, an arrogant bestselling novelist who has gone numb to his own life. Over one night he learns that the terminal and the people in it may be a simulation built by future humans who have forgotten how to feel, and that he only starts wanting to live when he is about to lose it.

## Genre and Tone
- Existential mystery thriller and liminal-space horror, written as a **novella of about 25,000–30,000 words**, in close third person on Declan. His inner voice is a novelist's: "Well. Here is something that is happening."
- **Darkly funny that turns serious** as the characters crack. Leo carries most of the dry humor.
- The dread comes from the unknown and from distrust inside the group, not from monsters. Creepy things happen, but there is no creature feature.
- **Quiet, wrong, and understated.** Herb prefers silence to spectacle. No voiceless announcements, no obvious winks such as the employee humming the song. Strangeness is plain and nobody comments on it.
- **The ending is bittersweet first, then warm, with a twist.** It is sad and then hopeful, with a final hook rather than an explanation.

## Comparables
- *Five Characters in Search of an Exit* (Twilight Zone): the direct inspiration.
- Sartre's *No Exit*, *Cube*, and *The Bar*: claustrophobic mystery with existential stakes.
- *Identity* (the John Cusack film): the group leadership shifts. The tough cop takes charge at first, and the main character takes over later. Herb wants to play that trope.
- *Predators*: strangers with common traits and sharp differences, trying to learn why they're all there.
- *Edge of Tomorrow*: an unlikable man is stripped down and becomes someone worth rooting for. The group races and fights toward a glimmer of hope.
- *Sphere*: objects drawn from their memories appear around the characters.
- *The Terminal*: the airport as limbo.
- *Into the Night* (the Belgian Netflix series): a sunrise deadline, with a backstory for each character.
- Star Trek TNG episodes in which aliens experiment on the crew.

## Setting
- A **new, nearly empty terminal at O'Hare**, hard to find and reached by rebooking. It is drawn from Herb's real experience of a strange Aer Lingus terminal. The group's flight is bound for **Dublin**.
- **Middle of the night.** The terminal is supposed to be closed until morning, so there is no staff and no flights. There are humming lights, **escalators stopped mid-step**, and **creepy, total quiet**. Herb does not want announcements, so earlier ideas of faint announcements nobody is making are dropped.
- The floors are mirror-bright and freshly mopped, and the arrivals leave no footprints.
- **The shutters come down** and the doors seal, and the windows stop being windows. A shutter coming down at night is alarming to everyone, because it should never happen. A bomb-shelter alarm is part of the sealing (carried over from session 1).
- **Outside is dark and unknown.** The danger builds from outside triggers: the TVs may still be on, then distant city lights, faint explosions, alarms, smoke seeping in, and news of aliens or a catastrophe. Herb wants different triggers that make the group feel imminent danger outside.
- Inside the terminal are the main gate area, a **gift shop, a restaurant and a bar glowing gold in the corner**. These are stages for the memory objects and clue props.
- **The Clarksville sign.** The gate display is blank all night. It flips to **"Clarksville"** just as the airline employee appears, and she acts as though it has always said that. The group says they are going to Dublin and asks where Clarksville even is. It becomes a creepy name for a place they seem to be headed. Nobody hums the song.
- **The clock:** the group believes something terrible happens at sunrise.

## The Experimenters and the Truth
- The experimenters are **future humans, who read as aliens**. They solved everything and have gone numb, and they run a simulation to relearn why life matters by watching people in crisis. It is not an end-of-the-world story.
- The five were chosen because each is already quietly stuck in life.
- They know every character's memories. Like the experimenters in *Sphere*, they place objects from those memories in the terminal to see which ones make a person feel, and some are frightening rather than tender.
- They also leave **clue props** (a maintenance key card, a boarding pass for a gate that doesn't exist, a luggage tag leading nowhere) that look like ways out and always dead-end. They also push the group to turn on each other.
- **The reveal comes in three steps:** unknown mystery, then the believed end of the world, then the sunrise truth that none of them are real and the simulation ends with the dawn.
- **The final twist (new in session 2).** Declan is actually one of the experimenters, a doctor or scientist lying in a pod, who was put into the simulation as a "video game" to live it. He wakes with the memories and, for the first time, feels alive. Anika in the simulation was an NPC, a character the simulation built from a real person. He rushes to find the real woman. He finds her with only a glimmer of the Anika he remembers, smiles, and thinks he might be able to make her feel. Herb wants this kept light: a final hook, not deep explanation.

## Protagonist: Declan Marsh, the Writer
- **Name:** Declan Marsh, as in chapter one. This replaces the placeholder "Eli."
- **Who he is:** a novelist whose first novel was a huge hit. He has written nothing in years because, having gotten everything he wanted, life feels boring.
- **Background:** poor, broken-home childhood. His success sits on top of deep insecurity.
- **Flaw:** arrogance as armor, and materialism. He name-drops, pulls rank and is a bit of a dick. He starts out hard to like, and Herb wants the reader to come around to him by the end.
- **Want:** to take charge and refuse the situation.
- **Need:** to feel again and to care about something. He must let go of control and accept what is true.
- **Leadership arc:** he does not lead at first. Ray leads, and Declan takes charge later in chapter two or soon after, when he spots the pattern Ray misses.
- **Voice:** smart and quiet, with dry, sarcastic lines. Under pressure he becomes panicky and unfocused. He narrates his own life in a novelist's phrasing, such as "A closed room was a sentence that had stopped."
- **His wound:** the one thing he truly loved was his childhood beagle. When it died he never let himself have another dog.
- **His ending:** he feels sad that his life is over. In this one night he truly feels alive, and he only wanted to live once he knew he would lose it. He fights, they escape the terminal and nothing changes, and he finally lets go as the sun rises.

## Key Supporting Characters
- **Anika (surname suggested: Castellanos), software developer and love interest.** Herb's pasted chapter spells her name "Anika," so that is the spelling used.
  - She is logical, functional and reflective, and she thinks the problem through when others panic.
  - Her calm is an armor. She built an AI from her late partner's messages and still talks to it. The armor drops in the quiet dead hours.
  - She meets Declan in the rebooking line and is unimpressed by his fame.
  - She dies near the end (suggested to line up with the sunrise fade). In the final twist, she is revealed as an NPC based on a real person, whom Declan seeks.
- **Leo (last name not yet established; "Okafor" was an earlier placeholder), lawyer and the clown.**
  - Funny and a bit stupid, and a surprisingly creative thinker. He is not a natural leader.
  - He carries a bottle of rare scotch from a defunct distillery, bought for his father, who died of a heart attack right before the trip. It reappears in the bar.
- **Hal Weng (placeholder), janitor.**
  - He is calm and unbothered. He is the voice behind the bar: "Evening. You folks are early."
  - He believes there should be no more flights that night and the terminal should be closed until morning. He is new to this wing. He was given the shift because the new terminal was opening, and he normally works elsewhere. His radio only crackles static. His master keys open every door except the shutters.
  - His first reaction to the sealed doors is mild: "Everybody calm down, there's probably an explanation, it's a new wing, maybe a malfunction."
  - He is a former hard-driving CEO who had a breakdown, lost his family, and chose to become a janitor. He may have kept his wealth. He is the wisest of the group and works out the truth first. Declan gradually recognizes his face from a magazine cover. He is Declan's mirror: a man who walked away from success.
- **Ray Dunleavy (placeholder), air marshal, in the windbreaker.**
  - He takes charge first, following the *Identity* trope. In chapter one he walks the shutter running a flat palm over the steel, checks the floor seam, and his hand leaves his jacket empty. Declan has seen men like him in the corners of rooms where something is about to happen. He has a wary, appraising look and says, "Sealed. Top and bottom. Somebody wanted it that way."
  - He is armed and suspicious, and the group wonders whether he is behind it all. A gift shop memento tied to something his wife always wanted cracks him. His deeper backstory is still open.
- **The Airline Employee, a she.**
  - A calm, ordinary-looking woman in an airline uniform with a robotic quality. She reacts to nothing and asks flat, curious questions like "Do you really think that?" She is the creepiest presence in the story.
  - She appears seated in the gate area, saying she arrived a few minutes ago and didn't see them come in. They didn't see her either. The Clarksville sign flips as she arrives.
  - Her flashback is written like a stranded traveler's, so we take her for a sixth passenger. The details drift toward a man who has everything and feels nothing, which is **Declan's own future if he never changes**. This fits the twist: she is the numb version of what Declan is outside the simulation. Her identity stays hidden until the end.
- **The beagle.** A drug-sniffing service dog that appears out of nowhere, creepy and sweet at once. It breaks Declan's armor. At some point it gets lost, which reopens his grief.

## Central Conflict and Stakes
- **External:** find an exit from a sealed terminal before sunrise, while the experimenters tease them with props that look like exits. Everyone distrusts everyone else.
- **Internal (Declan):** he must give up control and his armor and begin to care. Even though the escape fails to matter, caring is what makes the night real to him.
- **Stakes:** their lives, and the possibility that nothing is real. For the experimenters, whether anyone can still be moved. Declan's change may be what finally reaches one of them, the real Anika.

## Themes
- Meaning-seeking: people stuck together.
- You only learn to value living when you face losing it.
- Numbness versus feeling, and a life chosen versus a life drifted through.
- Real feeling can come out of an unreal night.

## Tastes and Influences
- Herb likes classic twist-driven stories and claustrophobic ensembles, and wants to avoid copying *The Mist*.
- He wants the page to do the strangeness quietly, with no winks and no hum.
- He likes cheesy, playful references (the Monkees' song) used for creepy effect.
- He likes character-trope subversion, as in *Identity*, and a Hollywood-warm ending with a twist and a hook.
- He wants to keep explanation light at the end.

## Three-act outline

## Act I: The Closed Room (about chapters 1–7)
1. **The seal (written).** Declan, Anika, Leo and the man in the windbreaker find the shutter down and the window gone. Ray checks the seams and says, "Sealed. Top and bottom." The room was empty a minute ago and now it is closed. A mop wrings out behind the bar, the floor is wet and unmarked by footprints, and a voice says, "Evening. You folks are early."
2. **Hal behind the bar.** The janitor believes the terminal is closed until morning. He is new to this wing, calm, and sure it is a malfunction in a new building. His radio gives only static and his master keys open everything but the shutters.
3. **The search.** They test every door and every stairwell. They find escalators stopped mid-step and total, creepy quiet, with no way out. They return to the main gate.
4. **Clarksville.** The gate board flips to "Clarksville" just as they notice a woman in an airline uniform seated nearby. She came a few minutes ago and didn't see them arrive either. Nobody saw anyone enter. Leo points out they are going to Dublin, and someone asks where Clarksville even is.
5. **Ray's command.** Ray takes the lead and orders everyone to empty pockets and bags. Suspicion spreads, especially toward Ray. Leo's scotch appears, and Declan and Anika bristle at being ordered about. Declan notices something Ray missed, a detail about the arrivals or the employee, and Declan wins the lead.
6. **The TVs.** A bank of TVs flickers on. The first news comes: strange reports, a city in trouble, something outside. Distant lights show at the edge of a shutter seam. The group senses imminent danger outside.
7. **The first prop.** A maintenance key card is found. Declan leads them to the service corridor and it dead-ends. The "exit" has been placed for them. Close of Act I: they realize someone is watching, and sunrise becomes the deadline.

## Act II: The Long Night (about chapters 8–16, midpoint in 12)
8. **Rebooking-line flashback.** Declan meets Anika in the line and she is unimpressed by his fame. A chapter of dry humor that shows the insecure boy beneath the novelist.
9. **The employee's questions.** The employee asks flat questions: "Do you really think that?" She unsettles each person differently. Declan's cover begins to slip.
10. **The bar and the gift shop.** The first memory objects appear. Ray picks up a gift shop memento tied to something his wife always wanted, and he cracks. Leo's scotch reappears behind the bar in a way he cannot explain.
11. **The beagle.** A drug-sniffing dog appears out of nowhere. It goes straight to Declan and breaks his armor. He tells no one why.
12. **Midpoint: the world ending.** Smoke seeps in, explosions are heard, and the TVs show news of aliens or catastrophe. The mystery becomes the end of the world. Declan stops resisting and starts caring about the group.
13. **The boarding pass.** A boarding pass for a gate that does not exist sends the group chasing a false exit. Suspicion splits them. Ray is accused, and Declan has to hold them together.
14. **Hal's face.** Declan recognizes Hal from a magazine cover and confronts him. Hal tells his story of the breakdown and the choice to be a janitor, and Declan sees himself.
15. **Anika's quiet hour.** In the dead hours Anika's calm drops and she talks to her AI of her late partner. She and Declan grow close. This is the Kochanski beat.
16. **The beagle is lost.** The dog disappears in the dark, reopening Declan's grief. His panic returns and Leo's humor cracks. Hal says what he has worked out: this is not real.

## Act III: Sunrise (about chapters 17–22)
17. **Hal's truth.** He lays out the theory: a simulation, made by someone who wants to see them feel. The group argues and rejects it, but the props and the footprints back it up.
18. **The employee's flashback.** Her story is told as a stranded traveler's, and the drift is clear: a man who has everything and feels nothing. Declan sees himself.
19. **The escape.** The group works as a team: Leo's creative idea, Hal's manual approach, Ray's skill and Anika's logic. They open the doors and step outside. (The exact method is still to be decided.)
20. **Nothing has changed.** Outside is the same night and the same unreal quiet. Anika dies or fades near the end of the night. The dawn comes whether they got out or not.
21. **Sunrise.** Everyone begins to fade. Declan feels sad that his life is ending, and only now realizes he wants more of it. He fights, then lets go.
22. **Waking.** Declan wakes in a pod as one of the numb experimenters, a doctor or scientist. He carries the memories and feels alive. He rushes to find the real woman behind Anika, a person the simulation copied. He finds her, sees a glimmer of Anika in her eyes, and smiles, thinking he might teach her to feel. Cut.

- How do the survivors get through the doors in Act III, and what do they see outside? We know they escape and nothing changes, but not the method or what outside looks like.
- Who is Leo's full name now? Chapter one gives only "Leo," so does "Okafor" stay as his surname, or should it be something else?
- Do you want to keep Hal and Ray as the janitor's and marshal's names, or does the chapter-one naming suggest alternatives? Their last names (Weng, Dunleavy) are placeholders too.
- Anika dies near the end. Does she die before sunrise, or does everyone fade together at dawn? Does Declan watch her go?
- How is the airline employee tied to the final twist, now that Declan wakes as an experimenter?

# Chapter 2: Early

### 1

The man who came around the end of the bar was somewhere past sixty, narrow through the shoulders, in gray coveralls faded to the color of dishwater. He had a mop in one hand and a yellow bucket on wheels in the other. He steered the bucket with his knee, with the ease of someone who had done it ten thousand times. A name patch over his pocket said HAL in red thread. His face was lined and mild and tired in a deep, settled way. Declan found himself looking at it a half second too long, with the faint, irritating tickle of a word on the tip of his tongue.

"Sorry," Hal said. "Didn't mean to startle anybody. I figured you were lost. They'll do that to you with the signs."

"Early," said Leo. "You said early. For what?"

"The morning flights." Hal glanced at the shutters and then, with no particular alarm, at the pale ceiling. "Terminal's closed overnight. Last departure was out of Concourse B around nine. They're supposed to open this wing at five for the Dublin run, and I'm supposed to have it clean before then. So you folks are early. By about five hours."

"We were told there was a flight," Anika said. "Tonight. Forty minutes from boarding."

"Huh," said Hal. He considered this with real courtesy, as though she had mentioned an unusual weather pattern. "Well. Somebody must've made a mistake."

"Somebody sealed us in," said Declan. "Did you see that? The shutters. Does that happen every night?"

"Not that I know of. I don't usually work this wing. I'm over in Three, normally. They put me on here tonight because it's new and nobody's broken it in yet." He shrugged. "It's a new building. Things malfunction. I bet there's a sensor in the ceiling somewhere that thinks it's on fire."

"There was a *siren*," said Leo.

"New sensors are very enthusiastic."

It was such a reasonable sentence, spoken in such a reasonable voice, that for a moment the whole room wanted to believe it. Declan could feel it, like a draft. Then the man in the windbreaker stepped between them and the bar, and the draft went out of the room.

"Sir, I'm going to need you to put down the mop."

Hal looked at the mop. He leaned it against the bar with great care, as if it might be offended. "Sure."

"Name?"

"Hal."

"Hal what?"

"Just Hal, on the shift sheet." The old man's eyes were steady. "What's yours?"

The man in the windbreaker took a leather folder from inside his jacket and opened it. He held it at the exact height of a man who had done it in a hundred airports and been ignored in ninety of them. "Ray Dunleavy. Federal Air Marshal Service. I'm working the Dublin flight."

"I *knew* it," said Leo, delighted, and then, hearing the room, less so. "I mean, I didn't know it. I noticed the arm thing. You keep touching your jacket."

"Habit," said Ray.

"So you're the guy," Leo went on, unable to stop, "who's supposed to be sitting in 14C in a fleece, pretending to read a thriller."

"It's a paperback," Ray said, without any expression at all. "Mostly I look at the other passengers."

He closed the folder. Declan noticed that he did not put it back in the same pocket. The gun was in there, then. He felt the relief of it again, and a sharper, less comfortable feeling underneath that he couldn't name. Someone in the room now had permission to be afraid for them, and it wasn't him.

"All right," Ray said. "Here's how this goes. Nobody panics, and nobody wanders off. We don't know what that was, so until we do, we assume it's a security event and we act like one. Hal, what keys have you got?"

Hal lifted a ring from his belt that was the size of a bread plate. "Master set for the whole annex. They gave it to me at the desk."

"What do you open with it?"

"Everything," said Hal. "That's what a master set is."

"Then we're going to find out." Ray looked around the group, once, one face at a time, the way you'd check a row of locks. "We're going to do a sweep. Pairs, always. Hal, you're with me. Ms.—"

"Anika."

"Anika, you take the lawyer."

"I have a name," said Leo. "It's Leo. It's on my card. Would you like a card?"

"No."

"I'll leave a few on the bar."

"And you," Ray said, and turned to Declan, "stay put in the main concourse, in sight of the board, and keep an eye on—"

"No," said Declan.

He heard it come out, and he heard it keep coming. "I'm sorry, but no. I'm not going to sit in a chair while someone who may or may not be a federal agent decides which of us is useful. I have people who are expecting me in Dublin. I have an agent, a publicist, a man from a *festival*—"

"Great," said Ray. "Then somebody's already looking for you." He did not raise his voice, and it hurt far more than if he had. "Come with us, then. Carry the flashlight."

Hal, who had said nothing, produced a small flashlight from his coverall and held it out to Declan, handle first, with a look in his face that wasn't a smile and wasn't far from one.

Declan took it. It was heavy, and warm from the old man's pocket. He had nothing to say about it at all.

### 2

They found out quickly that Hal's keys opened everything. They opened the gift shop, where the racks held a hundred identical snow globes of the Chicago skyline, each with a little tag that said **WELCOME**, and behind them a stockroom with boxes of unopened neck pillows. They opened the restaurant's kitchen, which was steel and spotless, with a row of unlit burners like a bank of closed eyes. They opened a utility closet and a family restroom and a service corridor whose floor was painted with a long yellow stripe that ran away from them and disappeared around a corner. Every lock turned with a clean, oiled click, as though each had been tested that morning.

None of them led out.

The service corridor ended in a fire door, and the fire door opened onto a concrete landing, and the landing was lit by a single caged bulb and bounded on the far side by another sheet of gray steel. Ray set his palm against it. He crouched to the seam, exactly as he had before. Hal tried six keys in a smooth, patient sequence, as if the shutter had a lock and the lock was merely being stubborn. It did not have a lock. It had no keyhole, no handle, no hinge, no panel, no manufacturer's stamp.

"Funny," Hal said. "Usually there's a stamp."

They tried the other end of the annex, and the other end had a shutter too. They tried the mezzanine, which they reached by a pair of escalators that were not running. They were frozen in the middle of their motion, with the steel steps pulled out into a staircase of gentle, regular teeth. Declan climbed beside Ray, one hand on a rail so clean that it squeaked. Halfway up he stopped. A pale shape hung motionless at the bottom of the nearest step, and he leaned closer to look. It was a child's mitten, small and blue, lying on the metal as if it had been dropped a moment before the power went. In a building that had never opened.

He said nothing. He wasn't sure why. He pocketed the mitten the way you'd pick up a coin on the street, and he felt Ray's eyes on his back, and felt that the look had measured something and filed it again.

The mezzanine was a glass gallery, shuttered. Below it the main concourse stretched away, as white and still as a photograph of itself. Hal's radio, clipped at his shoulder, produced a sound like a long breath over sand when he keyed it. "Maintenance, this is Hal in Nine," he said, and let go, and waited. The static went on. He tried it twice more, with the same mild courtesy, as though the radio might simply be taking a moment to compose a reply.

"What's the plan if nobody answers?" said Declan.

"Someone'll answer," Hal said. "Or they won't, and we'll wait till five. Either way it's the same."

"That's very Zen."

"It's very union."

"You're awfully calm for a man who's locked in a box."

Hal looked at him. His eyes were the kind that had been a different color once, before life had washed them. "I've been in worse rooms," he said.

Ray said, "Quiet," and it was quiet.

The silence in the annex was a new kind. Declan had been in quiet before. He had made a career of it, of sitting in a room until the quiet gave up something worth writing down. But this quiet gave nothing. It did not creak or tick or settle, and there was no distant traffic and no ventilation fan. When he walked, his footsteps took a long time to reach the walls and came back thin. He thought of the mop water, of the long even strokes, and how the floor had known him and kept no trace.

They came back down. Anika and Leo were waiting in the concourse with the grim expressions of people who had reached the same wall by a different corridor.

"Nothing," Anika said. "Three sealed exits. A fourth I'm pretty sure is just a mirrored panel. There's no vent big enough for a cat."

"I opened a pastry case," Leo said. "Empty. It's weirdly clean. I want you to know that I took it personally."

"Same," said Ray, flatly. "So that's where we are. We have a sealed building and no comms, and nobody on the other side knows we're in here, or they do and aren't saying." He let that settle. "Everybody back to the gate. We wait and keep our eyes open. If it's a glitch, it clears. If it isn't, somebody comes."

"And if nobody comes?" said Leo.

"Then we're a very well-trained group of people sitting in a chair."

Nobody laughed. They walked back across the great floor in a loose line, with their reflections going along beneath them like a second, quieter party, and sat down in the long row of chairs facing the departure board. The board still said **DUBLIN. Gate Z.** Under it, the row of dashes sat in its column and waited.

Ray did a thing then. Declan watched him do it, and watched the others watch. He stood in front of them, feet apart, and said, in a voice that carried the full weight of the badge, "Everyone, empty your pockets and your bags. On the seat in front of you. I'm not accusing anyone, but I'd like to know what's in here with us."

"Are you serious?" said Leo.

"I'm going first."

He was, too. He set a gym bag down, unzipped it, and laid out its contents one at a time on the row of seats: a spare magazine, a pair of handcuffs, a change of socks, a pill case with a daily label, a paperback with a cracked spine. He took the pistol from his jacket and set it down with the muzzle toward the wall, and for a moment it lay there under the humming lights, small and ordinary and very black.

There was a collective, involuntary silence, the kind that follows a clear and flat statement of fact.

Then, one by one, they did it too.

### 3

Anika's laptop, a cable, a battery pack, a tin of mints, a folded boarding pass. Leo's business cards, which he arranged in a fan out of habit, a flask he claimed was for medicinal purposes, and a very small stuffed rabbit he declined to explain. Hal's keys, his radio, a rag, a paper bag with half a sandwich in it. Declan's wallet, his passport, his phone, the heavy flashlight, and the small blue mitten, which he set down at the very end, a little behind the rest, hoping no one would ask.

Ray moved down the row, looking at each pile, with his hands behind his back like a man in a museum. He stopped at the mitten.

"Yours?"

"I found it."

"Where?"

"On the escalator."

Ray looked at it a moment. "Hold on to it," he said, and moved on, and Declan found that he was grateful and annoyed in equal parts.

It was then, with the whole row of their belongings laid out under the pale ceiling and the five of them standing around it like mourners at a very small wake, that Hal lifted his head and said mildly, "Well, I'll be."

They all looked where he was looking.

The board had changed. It had not flickered or blinked. One moment it had said **DUBLIN. Gate Z.**, and the next it said **CLARKSVILLE. Gate Z.**, in the same amber letters and the same calm typeface. Beneath it, where the dashes had been, was still a row of dashes, as though nothing had happened at all.

"That's wrong," said Declan.

"It's a typo," said Leo, in the voice of a man hoping to be told it was a typo.

"It's a different city."

"Clarksville," Anika said slowly. "Where *is* Clarksville?"

"Tennessee," said a voice. "I think. Or the other one."

They turned.

She was sitting at the far end of the row, in the last seat before the gate desk, with her hands folded neatly in her lap. She wore an airline uniform in dark blue with a pale scarf at the throat and a name tag that caught the light the wrong way, so that Declan couldn't read it from where he stood. Her hair was pinned flat. She had the ordinary, forgettable face of a person you would walk past in a hundred terminals and never once recall. She looked at the five of them with polite, mild interest, like a woman watching a pleasant film.

"Where did you come from?" said Ray. His hand had not gone to the gun, but his weight had shifted toward it.

"I came in a few minutes ago," she said. "I sat down to wait. I didn't see you come in."

"We were here," said Leo. "Right here. For an hour. In *those chairs*."

"Oh." She looked at the chairs with what seemed like honest regret. "I'm sorry. I didn't notice."

Declan had the cold, distinct sensation of a stair that was not there. They had not seen her either. He had sat in the middle of that row for an hour with his back to a wall and an eye on every approach, and Ray had walked the whole annex twice, and nobody had seen a woman in a blue uniform and a scarf take a seat at the end of a row.

"Ma'am," said Ray, evenly, "who are you with?"

"The airline."

"Which airline?"

"The one flying tonight." She smiled slightly. It was a very good smile. It was exactly the smile on the face of the woman on the safety card, and it rested on her face the way a sticker rests on a window. "I'm told there's a boarding."

"We're going to Dublin," said Declan. "That board said Dublin. It said Dublin thirty seconds ago."

"Did it?"
