# Story Bible, Session 1

## Working Title
**Working title: "Gate Zero"** (suggested placeholder; Herb will name it later)

Alternates (suggested):
- *No Departures*
- *The Quiet Terminal*
- *Hold Pattern*

## Logline
Five stranded travelers, rebooked into a silent, sealed-off terminal at O'Hare, must find a way out before sunrise. The man who insists on leading them, an arrogant bestselling novelist numb to his own life, slowly learns that the night, the terminal and the people in it may be a simulation built by future humans who have forgotten how to feel.

## Genre and Tone
- Existential mystery thriller / liminal-space horror, written as a **novella of about 25,000–30,000 words**.
- **Darkly funny that turns serious** as the characters crack. The lawyer carries most of the dry humor.
- The dread comes from the unknown and from distrust inside the group, not from monsters. Creepy things happen, but there is no creature feature (Herb avoided copying *The Mist*).
- It ends warm, in a Hollywood way, with a twist.

## Comparables
- *Five Characters in Search of an Exit* (Twilight Zone): the direct inspiration.
- Sartre's *No Exit*, *Cube*, and *The Bar* (the Spanish film about people trapped in a café): claustrophobic mystery with existential stakes.
- *Predators*: strangers with common traits and sharp differences, trying to learn why they're all there.
- *Edge of Tomorrow*: the arc of an unlikable man who is stripped down and becomes someone worth rooting for, plus the ending.
- *Sphere*: objects drawn from their memories appear around the characters.
- *The Terminal*: the airport as a place of limbo.
- The Belgian Netflix plane series (*Into the Night*): a sunrise deadline, with a backstory for each character.
- Star Trek TNG episodes in which aliens experiment on the crew like mice.

## Setting
- A **new, nearly empty terminal at O'Hare**, which is hard to find and reached by rebooking. It is drawn from Herb's real experience of a strange Aer Lingus terminal.
- **Middle of the night.** No attendants, humming lights, stopped escalators, faint announcements nobody is making.
- Once everyone has arrived, a **bomb-shelter alarm** sounds and the doors seal them in.
- **Outside is dark and unknown.** Herb chose this over a bustling airport seen through glass. Later there are distant city lights, faint explosions, alarms and smoke seeping in, and phones and TVs show news of aliens or a catastrophe.
- Inside the terminal are a **gift shop, a restaurant and a bar**. These become stages for the memory objects and the clue props.
- **The clock:** the group believes something terrible happens at sunrise.

## The Experimenters and the Truth
- The experimenters are **future humans, who read as aliens**. They solved everything and have gone numb, and they are running a simulation to relearn why life matters by watching people in crisis. Herb liked this. It is not an end-of-the-world story.
- The five were chosen because each is already quietly stuck in life.
- They know every character's memories. Like the experimenters in *Sphere*, they place objects from those memories in the terminal to see which ones make a person feel, and some are frightening rather than tender.
- They also leave **clue props** (a maintenance key card, a boarding pass for a gate that doesn't exist, a luggage tag leading nowhere) that look like ways out and always dead-end. These also push the group to turn on each other.
- **The reveal comes in three steps:** unknown mystery, then the believed end of the world, then the sunrise truth that none of them are real and the simulation ends with the dawn.

## Protagonist: The Writer (the Major analog)
Suggested name: **Eli Marsh**.
- **Who he is:** a novelist whose first novel was a huge hit. He has written nothing in years because, having gotten everything he wanted, life feels boring.
- **Background:** poor, broken-home childhood. His success sits on top of deep insecurity.
- **Flaw:** arrogance as armor, and materialism. He name-drops, pulls rank and is a bit of a dick. He starts out hard to like, and Herb wants the reader to come around to him by the end, as with Cage in *Edge of Tomorrow*.
- **Want:** to take charge and refuse the situation, like the Major who keeps trying to climb out.
- **Need:** to feel again and to care about something. He must let go of control and accept what is true.
- **Voice:** smart and quiet, with dry, sarcastic lines when he does speak. Under pressure he becomes panicky and unfocused, which contrasts with his usual numbness and with Mira's calm.
- **His wound:** the one thing he truly loved was his childhood beagle. When it died he never let himself have another dog.

## Key Supporting Characters
Names and ethnic backgrounds are suggested. Herb asked for a natural airport mix of backgrounds and genders and left the choices to the writer.

- **Mira Castellanos, software developer (the ballerina analog, and the love interest).**
  - She is logical, functional and reflective, and she thinks the problem through when others panic.
  - She is warm underneath, with an act of robotic calm over it.
  - She built an AI from her late partner's messages and still talks to it. The armor drops in the quiet dead hours, as with Kochanski in the helicopter scene of *Edge of Tomorrow*.
  - She meets Eli in the rebooking line, where she is unimpressed by his fame. She dies near the end.
- **Dez Okafor, lawyer (the clown).**
  - He is funny and a bit stupid, and a surprisingly creative thinker.
  - He carries a bottle of rare scotch from a defunct distillery, bought for his father, who died of a heart attack right before the trip. It reappears in the bar.
- **Hal Weng, janitor (the hobo analog).**
  - He is a former hard-driving CEO who had a breakdown, lost his family, and chose to become a janitor to escape his old life. He may have kept his wealth.
  - He is the wisest of the group, and his manual approach comes closest to escape.
  - Eli gradually recognizes his face from a magazine cover.
  - He works out the truth first, because he knows what a hollow life feels like. He is Eli's mirror: a man who walked away from success.
- **Ray Dunleavy, air marshal (the bagpiper analog).**
  - He is armed, seemingly worthless and a bit suspicious, and the group keeps wondering whether he is behind it all.
  - A gift shop memento tied to something his wife always wanted cracks him.
  - His deeper backstory is still open.
- **The Airline Employee (the weird thing out of nowhere).**
  - A calm, ordinary-looking person in an airline uniform who reacts to nothing and asks flat, curious questions like "Do you really think that?" It is the creepiest presence in the story.
  - Its flashback is written like the other passengers', so we take it for a sixth stranded traveler. The details slowly drift: a man who has everything, feels less and less, and finally feels nothing. It turns out to be **Eli's own future if he never changes**. Its identity stays hidden until the end.
- **The beagle.** A drug-sniffing service dog that appears out of nowhere, creepy and sweet at once. It stays for a long stretch and breaks Eli's armor. At some point it gets lost, which reopens his grief.

## Central Conflict and Stakes
- **External:** find an exit from a sealed terminal before sunrise, while the experimenters tease them with props that look like exits. Everyone also distrusts everyone else.
- **Internal (Eli):** he must give up control and his armor and begin to care, which is what lets him accept the truth and change.
- **Stakes:** the group's lives, and the possibility that nothing is real. For the experimenters, whether anyone can still be moved. Eli's change could matter to them too.

## Themes
- Meaning-seeking: people stuck together

# Chapter 1: Gate Without a Number

### 1

"You may have read my book," Declan Marsh said, which was his first mistake.

The gate agent did not look up. She had the face of a woman who had been told eleven times in the last hour that she was personally responsible for the weather in Newfoundland, and she kept her eyes on her screen with the stillness of someone defusing something.

"I'm sorry, sir?"

"*A Field Guide to Leaving.* It was, ah." He heard how it sounded and said it anyway. "It was on a number of lists."

The line behind him, which stretched back past the pretzel kiosk and curled around a pillar like a sleeping animal, made a sound. It was not a groan. It was something quieter and worse, the collective exhale of sixty tired strangers all arriving at the same opinion.

Declan was aware that there was a version of himself, a thinner and hungrier one from a long time ago, who would have been mortified. That version had worn the same two shirts in rotation for most of a year and had once eaten dinner out of a vending machine in a hospital waiting room because the vending machine was the only thing in the building that was free to stand near. But that version of him had not been available for comment in some time. What remained was the coat, which cost more than his mother's car had, and the watch, and the particular posture of a man who had learned that doors open for people who stand as if they have already walked through them.

"I'm sure it's wonderful," the agent said. "Your flight to Dublin was cancelled when the inbound aircraft diverted. I can put you on the six a.m. tomorrow, or—"

"I have to be in Dublin by noon. There's a keynote. There's a *prize*."

"Then I'd get on the six a.m., sir."

"Is there someone I can speak to who, I don't know, speaks?"

"Is it good?"

The voice came from behind his left shoulder. It was a level voice, with nothing in it that Declan could grab onto, and for a second he assumed it was addressed to someone else.

He turned. The woman standing there was somewhere in her mid-thirties, in a gray cardigan and sneakers that had clearly been selected by a person who had once been burned by a long layover. She had a laptop bag across her chest and a roller bag that she held in front of her like a podium. Her face was composed, almost professionally so, like a progress bar that had decided not to show you how close it was to done.

"I'm sorry?" Declan said.

"Your book. Is it good?"

"It sold four million copies."

"That's not what I asked."

Somebody in the line made a small, delighted noise. Declan felt the heat start up his neck, the old heat, the hospital-vending-machine heat, and he reached for the thing he always reached for, which was a sentence. Declan was very good at sentences. He had built a house out of them.

What came out was, "It's a novel about a man who can't leave his hometown."

"Okay," she said. "So I'll probably relate to it right now."

It wasn't even a joke. That was the problem. She delivered it with such mild, factual calm that there was nothing to push against. She wasn't mocking him. She was simply standing in the same line he was, in the same night, and declining to treat his name as a form of currency. He opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

"Next," said the gate agent, in the tone of a person who had just been handed a small gift.

Between them and the desk, a man in a very loud tie leaned back from the counter and clapped once. "Folks! If I may. As an attorney, I want you all to know that I have already drafted the complaint in my head, and it is *beautiful*." This was Leo, who had introduced himself to the entire line forty minutes earlier, unprompted, and who had since given out his card to three people who had not asked for it. "Honestly, it's like a poem. There's a stanza about the pretzel kiosk."

"Sir," said the agent.

"I'm going. I'm going, I'm a professional." He turned and winked at Declan on his way past, as though the two of them were co-conspirators in something. Declan, who had never conspired with anyone about anything, looked away.

The agent printed three boarding passes, then three more, then frowned at something on her screen and tapped it twice, as one might tap a fish tank.

"Okay," she said. "So this is odd. There's a flight to Dublin tonight after all. It's a late departure, out of the new international annex. They opened it last month, it's, um." She tapped again. "Terminal 9."

"There's no Terminal 9," said the woman in the cardigan.

"There is now. You'll want to follow the blue signs to the end of Concourse C, and then the gray signs. After that I think it's mostly a matter of faith."

"I'm sorry, did you say faith?" said Declan.

"I'm saying the signs get shy." She slid the passes across the counter. "Boarding in about forty minutes. Have a good night."

Declan took his. The gate field read **Z**, with no number after it, and in the line where the departure time should have been there was only a row of dashes, small and patient.

He looked at the woman beside him. She was looking at her own pass, with the same expression, and then she looked up at him, and for one second the progress bar slipped, and he saw, behind it, a person who was very tired and very, very awake.

"Anika," she said, and held out her hand, as if there were any reason to be polite to him.

"Declan."

"I know. You told the whole line." She said it kindly. That was the unbearable part.

### 2

The blue signs were easy. The blue signs went on for what felt like a mile, past shuttered Hudson News kiosks and a shoeshine stand with its chair tilted back as though somebody had just stood up from it, and a bank of charging stations where phones sat plugged in and abandoned, glowing, belonging to no one. Declan counted three before he made himself stop. It was nearly eleven at night, and the airport was doing the thing airports did at that hour, which was to quietly stop pretending to be for anyone.

Leo had attached himself to them somewhere around the pretzel kiosk and was talking about a case. It was a good case. A man had sued a cruise line over a buffet. Declan was only half listening, but the half that was listening kept noticing that the story was funny, and that Leo knew exactly where the funny parts were, and that this was a skill Declan had once had and could no longer locate.

"—and the thing is, the jury *wanted* to feel bad for him. They just couldn't get past the second helping." Leo spread his hands. "People think law is about truth. Law is about what the room can stand to believe. Hey, you're the book guy."

"I'm a book guy," Declan said.

"What are you working on now?"

It was the question. It was always the question. It came at him at dinners and panels and in the backs of cars, and every time it arrived it was a small cold hand on the back of his neck. What are you working on now. The honest answer was a document on his laptop titled *Untitled 2*, which had been open for twenty-six months and contained one sentence, and the sentence was *He woke up.*

"Something quiet," Declan said.

"Ooh. Love quiet. Quiet's the new loud."

"I wouldn't say that."

"Nobody ever does, that's how you know it's true." Leo flashed him a grin and turned to Anika. "And you? What do you do? Let me guess. Something with a spreadsheet."

"Close," said Anika. "I build software."

"What kind?"

"The kind people talk to."

"Oh, like Siri."

"Sure." She said it in such a way that Declan, who was not curious about anything, found himself looking over at her. There was a small hitch in the word, the sort you only notice when you've spent years making a living out of other people's hitches. It sat there a second. Then she shifted her bag on her shoulder and the moment closed over like water.

The gray signs began without announcement. One minute the corridor was the same airport as before, with the same carpet and the same low, expensive hum, and then they passed through a set of double doors and the carpet changed to polished concrete and the walls went to white. There were plastic sheets in places, hung off scaffolding. A moving walkway ran ahead of them into the distance, and it was off. Declan stepped onto it anyway, and the rubber felt strange underfoot, flat and dead, like walking on the back of something sleeping.

"This is how every horror movie starts," Leo said cheerfully. "I want that on the record."

"It's a construction wing," said Anika. "They always look like this."

"Is that a professional opinion?"

"It's an opinion. I've been in a lot of airports."

"Do you not feel any dread whatsoever?"

"I feel a lot of dread," she said evenly. "But it's mostly about the Dublin meeting."

Declan laughed. It was a short, startled bark, and it surprised all three of them. He couldn't remember the last time he had laughed at something that wasn't his own. He kept his face forward and walked faster.

Ahead of them, and he only noticed it because the corridor was so long and so still, someone else was walking toward the same distant light. It was a man in a tan windbreaker, maybe fifty, with a gym bag held close against his side and the loose, unhurried walk of someone who has spent a career standing in the aisles of moving things. He didn't look back. He didn't look at anything. But every few steps his right arm drifted up, as if by habit, and settled against the inside of his jacket, as if checking that something was still there.

Declan looked at that arm for longer than he meant to.

"Don't stare," Anika murmured.

"I'm not staring. I'm observing. It's a writer thing."

"It's a rude thing."

"They overlap."

At last the long corridor opened into a space so large and so quiet that it felt like walking into a held breath.

Terminal 9 was beautiful, in the way that expensive things are when no one has used them yet. The ceiling rose in a long pale curve, like the inside of a whale, ribbed with steel and lit from below. The floor had been polished to a mirror, and the three of them walked across their own reflections. There were rows of gleaming chairs bolted in long lines facing windows that looked out onto the black field of the airfield. There was a gift shop with its lights on and its gate down, and a restaurant with candles in glass jars on every table and no one at any of them, and at the far end, a bar, lit gold and empty and absolutely immaculate.

Above it all, the departure board glowed with a single line. **DUBLIN. Gate Z.** And then, in the column where a time should have been: a row of dashes.

"Okay," said Leo, softer now, "okay, I take back the horror movie thing."

"Where are the people?" Declan said.

It was a perfectly normal question and it landed oddly in the vast room. His voice went out and came back a little changed, as though the building were trying it on.

"Maybe they're not open yet," said Anika.

"For a flight that leaves in forty minutes?"

"They said it was new."

"They said it was *odd*."

The man in the windbreaker had gone ahead of them and taken a seat near the windows, one chair in from the aisle, with his bag between his boots. He sat very straight. He looked at nothing in the way people look at nothing when they are actually looking at everything. When Declan's eyes crossed his, he gave the smallest nod, the kind you give a stranger in an elevator, and returned to the window.

Declan chose a chair at the far end of the row, because that was what he did. He put his bag down, took out his phone, and discovered it had no signal. This was not alarming in itself; it was a concrete bunker of a wing. He put the phone away, then took it out again and looked at the time.

**11:47 PM.**

He put it away. He thought about Dublin, which was the only thing he'd let himself think about for weeks, because everything else behind it was a long gray hall. In Dublin there would be a lectern. There would be a thousand people who had loved him from a distance and a man from the festival who would say, kindly and with an eyebrow, *and we're all dying to hear what's next.* And he would stand at the lectern and say something about process. He'd become very good at saying something about process. It was nearly as good as having a process.

He was thirty-nine years old. He had everything he had ever wanted, in the way you have a house you've stopped walking through. He remembered, with a clarity that felt almost indecent, being nine and sitting on a fire escape in Youngstown, Ohio, holding a library copy of a book with its cover torn off and thinking, *if I could just get to the part where I'm someone, it will all feel like something.*

Well. He'd gotten there. It felt like waiting for a train.

"You okay?"

Anika had sat down two seats away. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the window, with her hands folded on her bag, and she had asked the question the way you'd ask a stranger on a bus if they had the time.

"I'm fine."

"You look like you're composing a eulogy."

"It's a reading voice. It's very sad." He paused. "Mine, I think."

There was a small, careful silence. Then she said, "I'll read it. The book. On the plane. If you'll tell me honestly if it's good."

"It's good," said Declan. It surprised him, how quickly he said it. "I mean, it was. I think it was. I don't know what I think anymore."

"That's an answer."

"It's a bad one."

"No," Anika said, and now she did turn to look at him, and her face did the thing again, the thing behind the progress bar. "It's the first honest thing you've said all night."

He didn't have a reply to that. He found, to his own considerable irritation, that he didn't want one. He wanted to keep sitting in the humming dark of a building that wasn't open, next to a woman who thought she was relating to a book, and not say anything at all.

Leo, across the aisle, had found a way to stretch out three chairs with his jacket as a pillow. "You two are very cute," he said, eyes closed, "and I'm going to need you to keep it down. I'm an attorney. I bill for dreams."

### 3

It happened at 11:58.

Declan knew the exact time because he was looking at his phone, scrolling through a dead inbox, and the screen changed first. One moment it said 11:58 and the next it said something that was not a time, a smear of numbers without a shape, like a word in a dream. He had just enough time to think *that's strange* before the sound came.

It started low, below hearing, a vibration in the soles of his feet and in the long steel ribs of the ceiling. Then it climbed. It was not an alarm in any of the ways he had ever heard an alarm. Fire alarms scream, and car alarms whine. This was a slow, broad, falling-rising note, one enormous tone sliding up and down like a hand on the neck of a cello, and it came from everywhere at once, from the walls and the floor and the polished chairs. He had seen it in old black-and-white films of cities with the lights off. It was a civil defense siren. It was a sound made by a country for a single, specific purpose.

"What the hell," Leo said, upright now, his jacket in a heap. "What is that? Is that a drill?"

"Nobody told us about a drill," said Anika, and she was on her feet, and the calm was still in her voice, but it had gone thin, like paper held up to a light.

The man in the windbreaker was standing too. His hand was inside his jacket, and for a moment, with the siren coiling and unspooling through the room, nobody looked directly at it. His eyes went from the windows to the corridor they'd come in by, and in the same breath he said, very quietly, "Get away from the glass."

"Why—" Declan started.

The shutters came down.

They fell from the ceiling in long steel sheets, with a sound like a guillotine being dropped one floor at a time. They dropped over the great windows first, one after another, and the black airfield and its distant blue runway lights vanished behind them panel by panel. Then, at the far end, with a crash that Declan felt in his teeth, the corridor they'd come through disappeared behind a slab of gray metal. He saw the moving walkway, still and pale, and then he saw nothing, just a wall that looked as if it had always been there.

The siren stopped.

The silence it left was enormous. It rang. Declan stood in the center of it with his phone still held out in front of him like an offering, and he realized he was breathing through his mouth.

Nobody said anything. The lights hummed overhead. The departure board glowed its single patient line. The candles in the restaurant burned, without any wind, with no one to have lit them.

"Okay," said Leo finally, in a small voice that had no case in it. "Okay. I want it noted that I said horror movie."

"It's noted," said Anika.

The man in the windbreaker had already moved. He walked the length of the shutter at the corridor's mouth, running a flat palm over the steel, then crouched and looked at the seam where the metal met the floor. He did it all without hurry, and Declan thought, with a cold little click of recognition, that he had seen men move that way before, in the corners of rooms where something was about to happen. When the man stood again, his hand left his jacket and came away empty, and Declan was ashamed at how relieved he felt.

"Sealed," the man said. "Top and bottom. Somebody wanted it that way."

"Who?" said Declan.

The man looked at him for a second, and there was something wary and appraising in it. It was a look that took a measurement and filed it.

"Fair question," he said.

Declan turned to the window, which was no longer a window, and then back to the room, and the room had changed. It was exactly the same room, with the same pale ceiling, the same shining chairs, the same bar glowing gold in the corner. But a minute ago it had been *empty*, and now it was *closed*, and he had not understood until just then how different those two things were. An empty room had a door. A closed room was a sentence that had stopped.

He thought, very clearly, in the voice he used for the first line of a chapter: *Well. Here is something that is happening.*

Then, from somewhere behind the bar, soft and rhythmic and unhurried, came the sound of a mop being wrung out into a bucket.

All four of them turned.

It was a wet, patient sound, a slow twist and a trickle, the sound of a man doing his job in a building that had just been sealed, as if nothing in the world had changed. Declan looked at the floor between himself and the bar, at the gleaming, unmarked, mirror-bright floor, and understood that it was wet. It had been mopped, recently and carefully, in long even strokes. And he had walked across it, and Anika had, and Leo, and the man in the windbreaker, and not one of them had left a single footprint.

The wringing stopped.

"Evening," said a voice from the dark behind the bar, mild and unsurprised, like a man greeting neighbors across a fence. "You folks are early."
