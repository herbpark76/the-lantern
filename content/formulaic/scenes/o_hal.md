## Hal

“Hal,” I said. “It's yours.”

He looked at me as if I had handed him a live animal.

“Mine.”

“You know the model better than I do. You know the Indonesian regulators. You know the Americans. Take it.” I stood up. “I'm going for a walk.”

The room went very quiet. Somebody's laptop chimed and they killed it as if it had sworn.

Hal followed me out to the elevators. He did not say anything until the doors were closed, and then he stood looking at the numbers going down.

“In eleven years,” he said, “you have never handed me anything.”

“That's not true. You gave a very nice toast at my wedding.”

He turned and looked at me properly for the first time all morning.

“Frank,” he said. “I wasn't at your wedding. You didn't invite me. Gabriella told me afterward it was because you wanted it small.” The doors opened on the lobby. “It was two hundred and ten people.”

He stayed in the elevator and rode it back up. I watched the doors close on him. He did not step back from them the way he had in Los Angeles. He stood there looking at me until they met in the middle.

## The Quay

I walked down to the lake and along the quay. There were swans, a great many of them, and a man selling roasted chestnuts out of a cart even though it was not cold, and a little boy throwing bread at the swans with real violence, as if they owed him money. I sat on a bench and watched a steamer come in.

It was the first time I could remember sitting down in the middle of a working day with nothing in my hands. I did not enjoy it. I would like to say that I did, and that I found some kind of peace on the bench by the swans, but I mostly felt like a man who has left the stove on in a house he no longer owns.

My number was 901.
