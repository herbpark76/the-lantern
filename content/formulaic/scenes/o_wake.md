## The Lake

I woke at 6:15, which is when I wake. For a few seconds that was all I knew, and they were very good seconds, possibly the best of my life. I did not appreciate them, because I did not yet know what came after.

Then I remembered the street sweeper.

I remembered all of it, in order: the pump that would not take my card, the man with the sunflower seeds, the voice that was nearer than the loudspeaker, my hand going to my pocket. I remembered lying on my back on Grand Avenue while the money walked away from me one bill at a time. I did not remember anything after that. I have been asked about it since, by people who are paid to ask, and I have tried to be accurate. There was no after. There was a drone, and then there was a ceiling.

It was not my ceiling. It was white, very high, with a plaster border of leaves running around the edge, the kind of ceiling a building puts in when the people who built it expected to be looked at for two hundred years. Gray light came in around curtains I did not own. Somewhere below the window a bell rang, a real one, and then something went past on rails with a long electric sigh.

I lay still and checked myself the way you check a car after you have hit a curb. There was no hole in me. I was wearing pajamas, which I do not wear, with my initials on the pocket, which I would never allow.

## The Watch

My father's watch was on my wrist.

I had pawned it on Vermont Avenue for nineteen hundred dollars and a yellow ticket. I had watched the man put it in a drawer. Now it was on my wrist, running, correct to the second, as it had been every day for twenty-five years except one.

I got up and opened the curtains, and there was a lake.

It was a large gray lake with mountains behind it, white at the tops, and a white steamer with a flag on its stern crossing it very slowly, as if it had been asked to. Along the near shore there were old trees and a stone promenade and a man in a red tracksuit jogging with a small dog under one arm, which seemed to defeat the purpose of both the dog and the jogging. I knew the lake. I had looked at it from hotel windows on perhaps thirty business trips, usually while on the phone to someone in another time zone. It was Lake Zurich, and I was looking at it from my own bedroom, in my own pajamas, with my initials on them.

The last time I had heard the word Zurich, it had been attached to my name, by people who were sure I had been there the night before. I had been sure I had not. I would like it noted that I was right about that, and that it did me no good at all.

My phone was on the nightstand. The date was the same date. I checked it twice. Under the time there was a number I did not recognize, in a clean gray font: 912. It was the kind of number that looks pleased with itself.

## Grand Avenue

I stood at the window and searched for my own death.

I typed *Grand Avenue Los Angeles shooting* with my thumbs, which were not quite steady, and got a farmers' market, a road closure for a half marathon, and a review of a sandwich place that the reviewer described as “honest.” I typed *Gaas Grand Avenue*. I typed *Port of Los Angeles attack*. The port, according to everyone who had written about it that morning, was having an ordinary day. One article was about a sea lion that had climbed onto a buoy and refused to get off, and had been given a name by the harbor patrol, and the name was Gary.

I typed *Gaas Dynamics*. The phone thought about it and asked whether I meant GAASCORE.

I typed my own name.

There was a great deal of me. There was a photograph of me at a conference in the mountains, in a navy sweater I have never owned, laughing at something with a man who had once been a prime minister. There was an encyclopedia entry. It said I was born in Connecticut, educated at St. Albert's and in Philadelphia, the son of the industrialist Malcolm Gaas (drainage pipe), and that I had founded GAASCORE in Zurich in 2009 with Harold Brenner. It said I lived on the lake with my wife, the cellist Gabriella Gaas, née Moretti. It did not say that I was dead. It did not say I had ever lived in Los Angeles. It did not mention a house made of glass.

## Henderson

I still knew the number of the house in Bel Air. I had never once dialed it myself, because there had never been anyone there I needed to call, but I knew it the way you know your blood type.

I went out into the hall to make the call. I did not yet know there was anyone to wake.

It rang four times.

“Henderson residence.”

It was a woman, older, a little out of breath, with a dishwasher going somewhere behind her. It was not yet ten at night in Los Angeles.

“I'm sorry,” I said. “I'm trying to reach the Gaas house.”

“The what, honey?”

“Gaas. On Stone Canyon. The glass house.”

“Oh, the glass one.” She laughed. “No, that's us. We've been here since '98. It was the Feldmans before. This is Carol. Who's this?”

I knew the name. That morning, in a kitchen in Bel Air, Gabriella had told me about a party on Saturday at Carol Henderson's, at their place instead of the club, because they had redone the pool house and wanted everyone to see it. I had said okay. I had known which Carol.

“Wrong number,” I said. “I'm sorry to have bothered you.”

“Oh, you didn't bother me. I'm just doing the dishes. We redid the pool house, so everything's a mess.” She paused. “You okay? You sound far away.”

“I'm in Zurich.”

“Well,” Carol Henderson said comfortably, “that'll do it,” and hung up.

## You're Up Early

When I came back into the bedroom I saw that the bed was very large, and that there was someone else in it.

Gabriella was asleep on her side with one arm under the pillow, the way she had slept when we were first married, before the house in Bel Air, before the guest room, before we started arriving at things in separate cars. Her hair was longer. There was a callus on the side of her left index finger, which I knew without having to be told was from a string.

She opened her eyes and looked at me standing at the window in my monogrammed pajamas.

“You're up early,” she said.

In Bel Air she said it every morning, with the same small pause in the middle, as if she were checking the line for a dial tone. Here there was no pause at all. She said it the way you say something to a person. Then she got up, kissed me on the shoulder on her way past, and went into the bathroom humming something, and I stood there with my hand on the place for some time.

“I have the run-through at ten,” she called through the door. “You don't have to come tonight. I know it's Jakarta.”

I did not know what Jakarta was. I said, “I'll be there,” because it seemed like the kind of thing the man in these pajamas would say.

In the hall, propped against the wall by the front door, there was a cello case covered in airline stickers. In Bel Air her cello lived in a humidity-controlled room that nobody entered. This one looked like it went places.

## Teodoro

At 6:40 the doorbell rang.

It was Teodoro. He was wearing the gray jacket of the building's concierge, with a little brass key on the lapel, and he was carrying a tray. From somewhere down the stairwell, very faintly, came a man talking quickly in Portuguese about what I took to be football.

“Good morning, Mr. Gaas,” he said. “Your coffee.”

In Bel Air I paid Teodoro. Here, as far as I could work out, the building paid him, and I paid the building, which is a different arrangement. He did not look at me the way he looked at me in Bel Air, which was carefully. He looked at me the way a man looks at a neighbor he likes well enough.

He held out the tray, and I looked down at the saucer, from above, which is where I had always been in relation to it.

![](/drawings/formulaic/saucer-4.svg)

*A saucer seen from above. The cup handle points at four o'clock.*

The handle pointed at four.

I drank it standing up in the hall in my pajamas. *The coffee was right.* It was exactly right. I have never in my life been so frightened by a cup of coffee.

“Your father telephoned the desk,” Teodoro said. “He said your phone is off.”

“It isn't.”

“He said that too.” He shrugged, as one man to another about fathers. “He will call again.”

## Dad

He went back down the stairs, and the radio swallowed him, and the phone rang in my hand.

The screen said DAD.

I had not seen that word on a screen since 2016. My father died that year at Cedars-Sinai, on a Thursday afternoon, while I was in the hallway outside his room on a call with Singapore. The last thing he said to me, an hour before, was, “Is it raining?” It was not. I told him so. He seemed disappointed. I never deleted the number. It was on a list of things to do, near the bottom, under the things that were urgent.

It rang a second time. Down the hall, Gabriella turned the shower on.
