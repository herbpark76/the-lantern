## Zurich

“What did you say to me yesterday morning?” I asked.

It came out wrong. Too fast, and in the voice I use to ask a chief financial officer where a number came from. I had never asked my wife a question in that voice. I had not asked my wife very many questions in any voice.

She looked up from her phone. “What?”

“Yesterday. When you came down. What did you say?”

“Frank, yesterday you were in Zurich.”

I was not in Zurich. I would have remembered Zurich. People do not forget Zurich. It is too expensive.

“I was here,” I said. “You came down at 6:52. You said I was up early.”

“You got in at one,” she said. “The car brought you from Van Nuys. I heard you. You stood in the bedroom doorway for, like, a long time, and I said ‘Frank?’ and you didn't say anything, and then you went and slept in the guest room. I figured you didn't want to wake me.” She put the glass down. “Are you okay? You look gray.”

I did not remember Van Nuys. I did not remember a doorway. What I remembered was this kitchen, this light, those words, and being pleased with myself about a pause.

I had built a company on a simple principle. When two numbers contradict each other, one of them is a mistake, and the mistake is usually somebody else's. I stood there for some time trying to decide whose mistake this was.

“Frank.”

“I'm fine.”

“Okay, well.” She turned her phone around and slid it across the marble to me. “Then you should probably look at this.”

## Guest Room

“One second,” I said. I left her holding out the phone and walked down the hall to the guest room.

The bed had been slept in. Only one side, the covers folded back in a neat triangle, which is how I fold them. At the foot of the bed was a small suitcase I did not recognize. On the nightstand was a chocolate wrapper from the airport in Zurich, folded into a tight square. I fold wrappers into squares. It is a habit from school. Nobody else in the house does it.

I stood there for a while. Then I unfolded the wrapper, which made it worse, and folded it again, and put it in my pocket.

When I came back to the kitchen, Gabriella was exactly where I had left her, still holding out the phone.

## Forty-One

**BREAKING · AP**
Federal investigators link Gaas Dynamics credentials to overnight attack on Port of Long Beach systems. CEO Frank Gaas sought for questioning.
*Gabriella's phone · 6:57 a.m.*

Later, when I opened the drawer in my study, there were forty-one missed calls on my own phone. I silenced it at ten o'clock every night. That was a rule, and I kept my rules. Eleven calls were from my general counsel. Three were from my chief financial officer. One was from my dentist's office, which I have never been able to explain. One was from a number with a 202 area code. 202 is Washington. Washington does not call to congratulate you.

I read the headline twice. Then I read it a third time, slowly, as if a third reading might make it say something different. It did not. They rarely do.

Gaas Dynamics made software that tells cranes where to put things. Ports, rail yards, warehouses the size of small towns. If the world is a machine for moving boxes, we were a small and very profitable part of its nervous system. So it was not absurd that someone had used our keys to get into a port. What was absurd was the someone. According to the phone, it was me.

I had never touched a Port of Long Beach computer system in my life. I am not sure I could have found Long Beach without a driver. The story was about a stranger who had my name and was wearing my face: the bad photograph from the Senate hearing, the one lit from below.

Behind me, Teodoro's radio said something in Portuguese, and then a jingle for what I believe was a mattress store.

## A Real Question

“What did you do?” Gabriella said.

It was the first real question she had asked me in two years. I knew it was two years because I remembered the last one. We were in the car, coming back from her sister's. She had asked whether I was happy. I had said, “Of course,” and then I had said something about the third quarter, which had been strong. She had said, “Okay.” I had thought the conversation went well.

She was looking straight at me now. She had stopped scrolling. Her thumb was resting on the edge of the phone.

“Frank. What did you do?”
