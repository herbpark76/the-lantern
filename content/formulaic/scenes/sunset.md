## Alone

{{alone}}

I drove east on Sunset with no music. Sprinklers. Joggers. A man hosing down the sidewalk in front of a restaurant that would not open for six hours. A billboard for a personal-injury lawyer whose face had been up there so long the sun had turned him pale blue. None of them knew who I was yet. Or they did, and were being polite. In this city it is often hard to tell.

## Motive

I tried to think like the man on the screens. If I had attacked the Port of Long Beach, why would I have done it?

Money was the obvious answer, and I threw it out at once. I already had the money. Revenge, then. Against whom? I could not name a single person at the Port of Long Beach. Boredom. That one I held onto for a few blocks. I had been bored for a long time without being allowed to say so, and boredom has made men do stranger things than shut down a port.

But I could not make it fit. I could not think of a reason. I have always been able to think of a reason. It is what I am for.

Somewhere around the Beverly Hills Hotel it occurred to me that there was another possibility. Someone else had a very good reason, and the reason was me.

I made a list in my head of people who might want to ruin me. It was longer than I expected and shorter than it should have been. Then I made a list of people who would know how. That one had four names on it. One of them was mine.

Hal Brenner, who knew where every dollar was. Sunny Rao, an engineer who had built half the scheduling system and whom I had fired in 2019, for reasons that seemed good at the time. Ira, who knew everything else. And me.

I took Ira off the list. Then I put him back on, because I could not think of a principled reason to take him off, and then I felt ashamed, and took him off again.

I stopped making lists.

## Corridor

My father died at Cedars-Sinai in 2016, of something slow. I visited him every day for the last three weeks. We discussed the Dodgers, whom neither of us followed, and the weather, and once, at some length, a nurse's shoes, which squeaked.

On the last afternoon he woke and looked at me for a long time. I thought he was going to say something. I had prepared, in case. I had a short statement ready in my head about what he had meant to me. It was quite good.

He said, “Is it raining?”

It wasn't. I told him so. He closed his eyes.

Afterward a nurse found me in the corridor and asked whether I would like a moment. I said, “No, thank you,” and asked where to sign. She gave me a clipboard. I signed in four places. My handwriting was perfect.

## Sunset and Doheny

The light at Doheny turned red. I stopped, because I am a man who stops at red lights. Even then.

The passenger door opened.

It was him. The bomber jacket. The grin. The young man I had been not quite seeing all morning, in the glass and on the wall and in the mirror. He got in, shut the door and buckled his seat belt, which surprised me more than anything else he did that day.

“Okay,” he said. “Okay. Drive. They're like two blocks back.”

He was perhaps twenty-six. His jacket came from a thrift store, and his hair had never met a professional. He smelled faintly of apples. He was out of breath, as if he'd been running to catch me, which made no sense, because I had been sitting at the light.

He put his hands to the vents. “Is the seat heater on? Why is the seat heater on? It's like seventy degrees.” He found the button and turned it off and seemed very satisfied with himself.

“Get out of my car,” I said.

“Light's green, Frank.”

I had not told him my name. The car behind us did not honk. A woman on the sidewalk looked straight through the passenger window, at the exact place where he was sitting, and kept walking.

I was not afraid, quite. I had the feeling you get when you hear a song from a long time ago and know every word before you can remember the title.
