## Denise

Ira's assistant answered. Her name was Denise, and she had been answering Ira's phone for as long as Ira had been my lawyer.

“Feld and Associates, this is Denise.”

“Denise, it's Frank Gaas.”

“Oh. Oh, okay. Hi, Mr. Gaas. He's— hold on. He's on the other line, but he said if you call— hang on, he's literally walking over right now. Hang on. I'm going to put you on a quick hold, okay? Okay.”

The hold music was a pan-flute arrangement of a song I knew but could not name. I listened to almost a full minute of it. I believe it was the Eagles.

## Ira

Ira Feld had been my lawyer for nineteen years. He billed in six-minute increments and was worth every one of them, which is a thing I have said about very few people and no relatives.

He was the only person in my life who was paid to tell me the truth. That made him the only person I fully trusted. I am aware of what that says about my life.

“Where are you?” he said.

“In the car.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

“Have you talked to anyone? Press, staff, anybody from the company?”

“No.”

“Have you deleted anything? Emails, texts, files. Anything since midnight.”

“Ira.”

“I have to ask. Somebody else is going to ask later, under oath, so I'd like to hear it first.”

“No.”

“Okay. Good. Don't go home. Don't go to the office. Don't touch your laptop. Don't call anyone, including Gabriella, including me on any other number. Are you listening?”

“I'm listening.”

“Are you driving right now?”

“Yes.”

“Then pull over. I don't want you rear-ending a school bus on top of everything.”

I pulled over, onto the shoulder above the reservoir. A woman jogged past with a dog in a small jacket. Neither of them looked in.

## A Process, Not a Story

“I've talked to the U.S. Attorney's office,” Ira said. “Briefly. They want you to come in. They'd rather you come in on your own than have it happen on the news. So would I. A man who walks in looks like a witness. A man who gets pulled out of a car looks like a defendant, and juries remember pictures.”

“Ira, I didn't do this.”

“I didn't ask you that, and I don't want you to tell me on this phone.” I heard paper moving. Ira still used paper. He said it made people nervous, and nervous people tell the truth. “Two o'clock. My office. We walk in together. You say nothing, I say everything. They'll take your passport, they'll do the paperwork, there'll be a bail number, we'll pay it. By tonight you're home, and this is a process, not a story.”

A process, not a story. I loved him for that sentence. It was the most soothing thing anyone had said to me all day, because it was a formula, and I have always known how to live inside a formula, as some people know how to live in hotels, and are a little lost in houses.

“Bring a jacket,” he said. “It's cold in there. They keep it cold on purpose.”

“Okay.”

“And Mr. G? Drive the speed limit.”

## Mr. G

Nobody called me Mr. G except Marcus at the front desk, and Ira had never met Marcus. Ira did not know the names of his own doormen. He considered it a form of integrity.

I did not mention it. I took the phone away from my ear and looked at it.

“Frank?” said the phone, in Ira's voice. “You still there? I think I lost you for a second.”
