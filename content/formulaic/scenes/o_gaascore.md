## The Tram

There was a car waiting downstairs, a long gray sedan with a driver who said, “Morning, Mr. Gaas,” and opened the door. I told him I would walk. We were both surprised, and he recovered first.

I walked along the lake and then up into the town, and when my feet began to complain I got on a tram, because it was there. I had not been on public transportation since a school trip to the Smithsonian in 1993, and I did not have a ticket. It did not occur to me that one was required. Nothing had been required of me in that way for a long time.

At the second stop a woman got on in a navy uniform with a ticket machine on a strap across her chest. She was in her sixties, with reading glasses on a beaded chain. She worked her way down the car, and when she got to me she held out her hand.

“Billett, bitte.”

“I'm sorry. I don't have one.”

She looked at me over the glasses. In Los Angeles, the day before, or a few hours before, or in some arrangement of time I did not yet have a word for, she had sold me sixty dollars of gasoline and told me there were no apples.

“No ticket, hon?” she said, in English.

The fine was a hundred francs. I paid it in cash from a wallet I had never seen before, which knew exactly where I kept everything. She printed something from the machine on her chest and tore it off.

“You want your receipt?”

“No,” I said. “Yes. Yes, please.”

She gave it to me and moved on down the car, and at the next stop she got off, and I watched her walk away along the platform until the tram took the corner and she was gone. I put the fine in my breast pocket. There was something already in there, folded, and I did not take it out to look. I knew what it was. I had not yet decided what it meant that it was there.

## GAASCORE

My company was in a stone building on the Bahnhofstrasse that had been a bank for a hundred and forty years and was still a bank, in the sense that mattered. Over the door, in brushed nickel, very discreet, it said GAASCORE.

I knew what we did before I was through the revolving door. I knew it the way you know the layout of a house in a dream: completely, and without ever having learned it.

GAASCORE took everything a person had ever done with money, and a number of things they had done without it, and turned it into a single number between 300 and 1000. Banks used the number. Landlords used it. Employers, insurers, and two governments that I preferred not to think about used it. If your number was above 800 the world opened its doors a moment before you reached them. If it was below 600 you found that a great many doors were heavier than you remembered. People checked their number the way people used to check the weather. That had been my idea, the lock screen. It was the best idea I ever had, and at that moment, crossing the lobby, I would have given a great deal to have had a worse one.

Mine was 912. The young man at the security desk glanced at his tablet as I went by and straightened up a little, and I understood that he had looked at my number before he looked at my face.

## Morning Ops

Morning Ops was at nine, on the top floor, in a room with a long table and a view of the lake. I knew where I sat. Everyone else knew too.

Ruth read the numbers. In Los Angeles I had sat through perhaps two thousand of these meetings and could not remember a single number Ruth had ever read. Here I heard every one of them. Accounts scored overnight, 211 million. Average movement, plus point four. Disputes filed, 38,000 and change, of which the system had resolved 37,900 and change before anyone in the building was awake. A small cluster of anomalies in Lyon that turned out to be a wedding. Ruth read each number in the same calm voice, and I sat there and received them, one after another, the way a man who has been very thirsty receives water, and somebody asked me if I was all right.

Hal Brenner sat on my right. In Los Angeles, Hal was my chief financial officer, who had given a very good toast about loyalty at my wedding and later signed the statement that put me on leave. Here he was my co-founder. His name was on the door downstairs, under mine, in slightly smaller letters, and from the way he looked at me when I came in I gathered he had never forgiven the font.

“Jakarta,” Hal said.

## Jakarta

Sunny Rao ran Risk. In Los Angeles I had fired Sunny in 2019, in a conference room, with a lawyer present, for reasons that seemed very important to me at the time. Here there was a small plant on Sunny's desk that I had apparently given as a gift, with a card still stuck in the soil that said *Well done* in my handwriting.

Sunny put it up on the screen.

Overnight, the model had downgraded forty thousand people in Jakarta, nearly all of them below 600, which was the line under which the Indonesian banks froze accounts. Most of them drove motorbike taxis. By six in the morning local time there were people outside the banks on Jalan Thamrin holding up their phones so the cameras could see their numbers. By eight the cameras had found out whose number it was.

“It's not a bug,” Sunny said. “I want to say that first. Everyone is going to ask if it's a bug. The model did exactly what it's built to do.”

“Then why?” I said.

“Because they all did the same thing on the same night.” Sunny clicked. A column of transactions came up, identical down the screen. “Forty thousand people who have never met each other made the same small payment at a fuel station between two and three in the morning. Sixty thousand rupiah each. Most of them at 2:51.” Sunny looked at me. “The model has never seen anything like it. It decided they were one person.”

Nobody said anything. On the lake a steamer went by, very slowly, with its flag out.

I took my phone out under the table and looked at the lock screen. 909. I had not done anything yet, and I had already lost three points.

“We need a position by ten,” Hal said. “Before the Americans wake up.”

Everyone at the table turned to me. I had spent my whole life in rooms where that happened. It had never once been a question before.
