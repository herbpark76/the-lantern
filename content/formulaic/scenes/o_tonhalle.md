## Elgar

I have paid for a great many concerts. I have sat in the front row of a great many of them, in a good suit, next to a person I was hoping to acquire, and thought about something else for two hours and stood up at the end before most of the room. I had never once, until that night, listened to one.

The hall was white and gold, with chandeliers like frozen fireworks and a ceiling painted with women who appeared to be getting on very well with some clouds. A man three rows in front of me coughed, and then coughed again, in time with the music, which I thought must be either an illness or a very particular kind of courage. Gabriella was in the front of the cellos, on the left, in black. I watched her hands. I did not watch the conductor, who was very energetic and whom I gathered from the program was famous.

When she played the eight bars I had heard through the door that afternoon, she played them a way she had not played them in the apartment, and I understood that she had been looking for it all day and had found it just then, in front of fifteen hundred people. Nobody else in the room knew. I am fairly sure I was the only person in the hall who had heard the other versions. It was a very strange feeling to be the only person who knew something good about someone.

At the interval I went out into the foyer and looked at my phone.

742.

My accounts were the same as they had been at six that morning. It was as if something had decided, very calmly, without any evidence, that I was turning into somebody else.

## The Steps

After the concert I waited outside on the steps while she packed up. The lake was black and there were lights on the far shore, and a last steamer was coming in with its windows lit, very slowly, the way they all did, as if they were paid by the hour.

A man sat down on the step next to me.

He was older than me, in his late fifties, a Black man with gray coming in at the temples, in a good overcoat that had been good for a long time; the cuffs had gone soft. He had a small notebook open on his knee and a pencil, and the page was full of numbers, in columns, very neat, with some of them circled. He did not look like he had been at the concert. He did not look like he had been anywhere in particular. He looked like a man who had been sitting on that step for some time and had simply moved over to make room.

He glanced at the phone in my hand, at the number on the lock screen, and then back down at his notebook, and made a small mark.

“You're early this time,” he said.

He had a calm voice, a little hoarse, the voice of someone who does not use it often and does not waste it when he does.

“Early for what?”

He closed the notebook and put the pencil in the spine and stood up, slowly, with one hand on his knee.

“Usually,” he said, “it takes you a lot longer to notice the coffee.”

He started down the steps toward the quay. I stood up.

“Who are you?” I said. It was the second time I had asked somebody that in a day. It came out sounding like a deposition.

He did not turn around. “Abraham,” he said. “Abe, if we're going to keep running into each other.” And then the crowd from the hall came out around me, all at once, talking and buttoning their coats, and when it had gone past, so had he.

## The Receipts

Gabriella came out with the cello on her back, in its case with the airline stickers, and took my arm.

“Who was that?”

“I don't know,” I said, which was true.

We walked home along the lake. I bought a ticket for the tram, at the machine, which took me three attempts and some help from a teenager, and when the inspector got on at Bellevue I held it up before she asked. It was a different inspector. I checked.

When we got in, Gabriella went to bed, and I stood in the hall in the dark and took everything out of my breast pocket and put it on the table under the lamp.
