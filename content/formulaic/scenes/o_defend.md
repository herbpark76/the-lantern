## The Statement

“The model is the model,” I said.

I heard the voice come out of me, the good one, the one I keep for senators and for the second half of earnings calls, and the room relaxed the way a room does when it hears the voice it was waiting for. Hal nodded slowly. Somebody started typing before I had finished the sentence.

We wrote the statement together. I dictated most of it. It said that GAASCORE did not make decisions, it described them; that the number was a mirror and not a judge; that we understood the distress of the people affected and would be reviewing individual cases through the usual channels, which had a link. It was very good. It was the best statement I have ever written, and I have written a great many, and I knew while I was writing it that every word was true and that it was a lie.

It went out at 9:40.

By eleven the company was up four percent. By half past eleven my number was 931. It went up. I would like that noted. I had never in my life been rewarded so quickly for something I was so sure was wrong.

## Thamrin

There was a television in the lobby on my way out, tuned to a business channel with the sound off. It was showing Jalan Thamrin. A man on a motorbike had stopped at the edge of the crowd and was holding his phone up to the camera, very steadily, the way you would hold up a photograph of someone missing. The number on it was 588.

He was middle-aged and solid, with a bad knee, or with the stance of a man who had one. He looked a great deal like the man who had worked our front desk in Los Angeles for six years, whose name tag I had never once read correctly. I stood there until the channel cut away to the price of copper.

By the time I reached the street, my number was 904.
