## The Fiat

She drove. Her car was a small red Fiat with a dent in the passenger door and a child's car seat in the back with nobody in it, and a parking ticket on the dashboard that she moved onto the floor so I could sit down. She drove the way she talked, steadily and a little faster than I expected, up the hill through streets that got quieter and greener as they climbed.

“I should tell you,” she said, “that I don't believe in this kind of thing.”

“What kind of thing?”

“I don't know yet. That's what worries me.”

## The Ward

The clinic was an old villa with a newer building behind it, joined by a glass corridor. Inside it smelled of floor polish and soup. The woman at the desk was large and calm and wore a cardigan with a cartoon cat on it, and she looked at Mia, and then at me, and then at the screen in front of her.

“He's gone,” she said.

“Gone where?”

“Out. Walked out.” She checked something. “We noticed at eight thirty-one. I sent you a message.”

Mia took out her phone, and looked at it, and put it away again without saying anything.

His room was at the end of a corridor on the second floor. The door was open. There was a bed made badly, the way a young man makes a bed when someone has told him to, and a window open onto the garden, and a smell I knew before I could name it. It was apples.

On the bed, laid out flat, as if somebody had been about to pack it and changed his mind, there was a cheap bomber jacket from a thrift store. In the wastebasket there was a banana peel.

“He was here at breakfast,” Mia said from the doorway. “I sat with him. He ate two bananas and asked me if I was happy.” She folded her arms. “Nobody asks me that.”

I picked up the jacket. It weighed almost nothing. There was something stiff in the inside pocket, and I took it out, and it was a yellow pawn ticket, folded once. Thirty days. The name on it was mine.

“Do you know what that is?” Mia said.

“Yes.”

“Are you going to tell me?”

I thought about it. I thought about Grand Avenue and the bills going off along the asphalt one at a time, and the ticket going with them, and I thought about how that would sound in a room like this one, to a woman like this one, whose job was to listen to how things sounded.

“Not yet,” I said.

She looked at me for a while. Then she nodded, as if *not yet* were an answer she was used to and did not mind.

“You can take the jacket,” she said. “Nobody here is going to claim it.”

I put it back on the bed. I kept the ticket.
