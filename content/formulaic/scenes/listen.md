## The Radio

I drove. He turned on the radio. I have never let anyone touch the radio in my car. I did not stop him.

He went through four stations, complaining about each one, and landed on a song I had not heard since I was fifteen. He sang along, badly, knowing every word. I had known every word once, too. I had played it in my room with the door closed, very quietly, because music in my parents' house was something you listened to at the symphony, in a suit.

“So you're wondering why everybody keeps saying the same thing to you,” he said.

“I'm wondering who you are.”

“{{name}}. Hi. And you're wondering why everybody keeps saying the same thing to you.”

He was right. I hated that he was right.

“How do you know my name?”

“Dude, your face is on every screen in the city.”

That was a reasonable answer. I had not expected a reasonable answer, and it annoyed me more than anything else he had said.

## The Music Room

“When's the last time you heard her play?” he said.

I did not ask who he meant. I knew exactly who he meant, and he knew I knew. We rode along Sunset for a while without anybody saying anything.

“I don't remember,” I said.

“Sure you do.” He turned the radio down, not off. “You were on a call. She was playing with the door open. You walked over and closed it. Really gently. You didn't want to interrupt her.”

I had never told anyone that. I had never told myself that, in so many words.

“I was on with Singapore,” I said.

“I know. It was a big call.” He didn't say it unkindly. He said it like a fact, like the weather in Singapore.

## Composure

“Can I tell you what I think?” he said. “I don't think the world's repeating, Frank. I think you're just finally listening to it.”

“That's a fortune cookie.”

“Composure, Frank,” he said, in my father's voice, and went back to the radio.

There was no performance in it. It was the voice from the dining room, with the fish and the place cards and the tablecloth being changed around a seven-year-old boy. It came out of the mouth of a young man in a thrift-store jacket, and then it went back wherever it lived.

I have never told anyone about my father's voice. I have never told anyone about the water glass.

He turned the radio back up. It was a commercial for a mattress store.

“Hey,” he said, “do you still know the words? To that song?”

“No,” I said.

That was a lie. He knew it was a lie. He let me have it.
