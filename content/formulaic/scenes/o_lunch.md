## History

On the way to lunch I called Sunny.

“I need you to pull something for me,” I said. “My own score. The whole history.”

There was a short pause, the pause of a person deciding whether a request is a test. “Yours?”

“Mine.”

“Give me a minute.” Keys. “Okay. It's flat. It's been flat for six years. You're the flattest line in the building, Frank. Nine-twelve, nine-twelve, nine-twelve. We used to put it in the investor deck.” More keys. Then nothing.

“Sunny?”

“That's weird.”

I stopped on the pavement. A woman with a pram went around me, saying something in Swiss German that I took to be a judgment.

“There's a drop. One point. At nine-fourteen.” Sunny read it out carefully. “That's weird too. It's stamped in Pacific time. We don't stamp anything in Pacific time. Nine-fourteen a.m., Pacific. Nothing before that for six years, and then one point, and then it just keeps going. A point here, two there. No transactions behind any of it. The model just—” Sunny stopped. “It's like it's lost confidence in you. That's not a technical term. I'll find the technical term.”

Nine-fourteen. I had looked at the clock on the dashboard on Grand Avenue, just before the loudspeaker. It had said 9:14, and I had been early for Ira by nearly five hours, or I had been early for nothing at all. I had not known, until Sunny said it, that I had remembered the time.

“Frank? Do you want me to escalate it?”

“No,” I said. “Just watch it for me.”

“Watch it do what?”

“I don't know,” I said. “Whatever it's going to do.”

## The Window

On the way to lunch I passed a jeweler's.

It was a narrow shop between a bank and another bank, with one window and nothing much in it, which on the Bahnhofstrasse is how you know a shop is serious. On a square of gray velvet in the middle of the window there was a single watch.

It was my father's watch. It was the same model, the same face, the same slightly worn crown, the same small scratch at seven o'clock where I had once caught it on a car door in Montecito. There was a card beside it, handwritten, that said *Pre-owned* and gave a price in francs that would have bought a house in most of the places I have ever lived.

I looked at my wrist. The watch was there. I looked at the window. The watch was there too. I could account for one of them.

Behind the glass, at the counter, a man was eating sunflower seeds out of a paper bag and spitting the shells into a cup with great accuracy. He looked up and saw me, and he held up one finger, and then nine, and then he went back to his seeds. I walked on. I did not go in. I have thought about that a good deal since.

## Lunch

I had lunch at a restaurant where the paintings on the walls were real and the waiters were not friendly, and both facts were the point. They knew me. There was a table that was mine, in a corner under a small blue painting of a woman that someone had once told me was worth more than the building, and the waiter brought me a glass of mineral water without asking and said the veal was good today, in a tone that suggested it had been good every day for eighty years and would not be thanked for it.

I ordered the veal. I took out my phone. 896.

I had done nothing since the last time I checked. I had walked down a street and looked in a window. It occurred to me that the number did not need me to do anything. It had its own idea of where I was going, and it was getting there without me.

## Mia

A woman sat down across from me.

She did not ask. She pulled out the other chair and sat in it and put her bag on the floor and pushed a strand of hair out of her face, and I had the impression she had been walking fast for some time. She was in her early thirties, in a dark coat with one button that did not match the others, and her hair was coming loose from a clip that had clearly been asked to do too much. Her eyes did most of the work in her face. They were tired and very steady.

“You said you'd come,” she said.

“I'm sorry—”

“Mia. Mia Ritchie.” She waited. “You said today.”

I have a very good memory for people who might want something from me, which is to say everyone, and I had never seen her before in my life. The waiter appeared, took in the situation, and withdrew with the air of a man who has seen a great many things at that table and will be taking all of them to his grave.

“I'm a psychiatrist,” she said. “At the clinic on the Zürichberg. I'm not here as your doctor.”

“You're not my doctor.”

“No.” She frowned, as if she had heard herself from a little way off. “Sorry. I don't know why I said that.” She took a breath. “There's a young man on my ward. He came in four days ago. No papers, no wallet, and a name he won't keep to. He's given me three. Every morning he asks for you. By name. He says you'll know him. He says you owe him a lemon tree.”

She said it all very evenly, in the voice of a person reporting something she has decided not to have an opinion about yet. Then she reached into her coat and took out a small curled piece of paper and put it on the tablecloth between us.

“He asked me to give you this. He said you'd want it back.”

It was a gas station receipt. Thermal paper, the kind that fades. Sixty dollars, pump four, 8:31 a.m.

I did not pick it up. I put my hand to my breast pocket instead, and felt through the cloth the tram fine, and behind it the other thing, the folded thing I had not taken out to look at. I did not need to take it out. It was the same receipt. I knew where it had been creased.

“Mr. Gaas?” she said. “Do you know him?”

I looked at the receipt on the table and the woman across from it, who was looking at me the way I imagine she looked at people on her ward, with great patience and no conclusions, and behind her, through the window, the Bahnhofstrasse went on being expensive in the sunshine.
