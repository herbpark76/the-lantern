## Normal

I decided to be normal. Normal is a discipline, and I am a very disciplined man.

I showered. I chose a gray suit, because gray is the color of a man with nothing to explain. I kissed Gabriella on the temple, which is where I kiss her, and she let me, which is what she does. I said I would be home by seven. I am always home by seven. Then I drove to Century City, where Gaas Dynamics occupied forty floors of a tower with my name on it, in brushed nickel letters I had approved personally from a choice of eleven typefaces.

At seven I took my phone out of the drawer in the study and put it in my pocket, face down, without looking at it. In the car I did not turn on the radio. Some part of me already knew there would be something on both.

At the light on Santa Monica a man in a Prius beside me was eating cereal out of a mug while he drove. He had a spoon and everything. I thought about him for several blocks. I still wonder where he was going that he could not eat first.

## Every Screen

Every screen in the lobby was showing my face. It was not a flattering photograph. It was the one from the Senate hearing, lit from below, which makes any man look like he is telling a ghost story about himself.

Under the face the words crawled from right to left: PORT OF LONG BEACH. GAAS DYNAMICS. SOUGHT FOR QUESTIONING. The words did not look at me. They were busy.

Nobody in the lobby looked at me either. People were looking at the screens. I stood under eleven of my own faces and was, for perhaps four seconds, the most invisible man in Los Angeles.

Except to one person. A young man by the revolving door, in a bomber jacket much too cheap for the building, was looking straight at me and grinning. Then the door turned, and he was outside, or he was not anywhere.

## Desmond

Marcus had worked our front desk for six years. He had a daughter at UCLA studying something with “bio” in it, and a bad knee he blamed on high school football or the Army, depending on the day. He called me “Mr. G,” which nobody else was permitted to do. I permitted it because it made me feel like a man whose doorman likes him.

Every Christmas I gave him an envelope. Every Christmas he said, “Mr. G, you didn't have to,” and I said, “I know.” That was the whole relationship. It was one of the better ones I had.

He looked at me. Then at the screens. Then back at me. He picked up the desk phone, put it down, and picked it up again.

“Uh. Sir,” he said. “Can you, um. Can you just hang on one second? Just right there. I just gotta—” He pointed at the phone, as if the phone would explain.

Behind the desk a printer woke up, printed a single page, and went back to sleep. Nobody went to get it.

## Hal

Across the lobby an elevator opened, and Hal Brenner stepped out. Hal was my chief financial officer. He had been with me for eleven years. He had been at my wedding. He had given a toast there about loyalty, which people still mentioned.

He saw me. I saw him see me. He had his phone in one hand and a coffee in the other, and for a moment he simply stood there in the elevator doorway with both hands full.

Then he stepped backward into the elevator, and the doors closed, and I watched the little numbers above them go up, and up, and stop at forty.

One of the three calls from my chief financial officer had come in at 4:12 a.m. I had not listened to the message. I still haven't. Ira has it now.
