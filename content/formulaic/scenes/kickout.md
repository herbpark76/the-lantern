## The Bus Lane

I pulled into the bus lane and reached across him to open his door. That is not a thing I do. I have people who open doors. But there are moments when a man needs to put his own hand on something.

He didn't move. He was turned around in his seat, looking out the back window with great interest.

“I mean, you can,” he said. “Totally your call. But then you're by yourself in a very recognizable car on, like, the most photographed street in the world. I'm just saying.”

“Who are you?”

“Can we do that part while we're moving?”

## Synchronized Swimmers

Three black Suburbans turned onto Sunset behind us in perfect formation, like synchronized swimmers. They did not hurry. They did not have to. Everyone in Los Angeles was going to tell them where I was.

A bus pulled up behind us and honked. A long one. The driver had a very reasonable point.

“I'm {{name}}, by the way,” he said. “You're welcome.”

“For what?”

“For the next ten minutes.” He thought about it. “Maybe fifteen.”

I closed his door. It was not a decision; my body did it before I was consulted. My body, it turned out, had opinions, and had been keeping them to itself for forty-three years, like a good employee, right up until the morning he doesn't.

“What kind of name is that?”

“Mine,” he said. “What kind of name is Frank?”

“My grandfather's.”

“See,” he said, as if that settled something, and turned back around to watch the Suburbans.

I pulled out in front of the bus. The bus driver gave me a look I have not received since boarding school.

He was asleep within two blocks, or pretending. His mouth was a little open. He looked younger asleep. I found myself driving more smoothly, for his sake, which made no sense at all.
