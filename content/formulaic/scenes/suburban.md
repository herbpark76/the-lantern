## Three Hundred Feet

The driveway was three hundred feet of heated limestone. I had driven it perhaps four thousand times. I had never once walked it.

Walking is slow. When you walk, things can watch you coming.

The limestone was warm through my shoes. I had paid to have it heated so it would never ice, in a city where it does not ice. I spent most of the first hundred feet on that. It is a strange thing to discover at forty-three that you have spent a great deal of money protecting yourself from weather that was never going to come.

Across the road, a young man in a bomber jacket was sitting on my neighbor's wall, eating an apple and watching me walk. When I looked again he was gone. My neighbor's wall is eleven feet high. I know because I paid for half of it, after a dispute about the peacock.

Halfway down, I noticed I was walking like my father walked into board meetings: chin level, hands empty, as if whatever waited at the end had been scheduled by him.

## Four Inches

The rear window came down four inches. Behind it was a man in sunglasses, although the sun was not really up yet. He had the kind of face you could not describe to a sketch artist. I know, because later I tried. The artist kept drawing perfectly good faces, and none of them were his.

“Mr. Gaas,” he said. “You're up early.”

Same pause before “early.”

“This is a private road,” I said.

“Yeah, I know. Sorry. We'll be out of your way in a second.” He sounded tired. He sounded like a man at the end of a night shift, which I suppose he was. “Somebody asked me to drop this off. I don't know what it is. I don't ask.”

“Who asked you?”

“Honestly? I get a text with an address. That's the job.” He shrugged. “I'm not police, if that's what you're worried about. That's later. That part's going to be a lot louder than this.”

He held a white card up to the gap. It was blank except for two numbers, written by hand in blue ink.

*A blank white card with two numbers written by hand in blue ink: 34.0522 and 118.2437.*

“For later,” he said.

From the front seat, someone I couldn't see said, “Ask him about the parking,” and the man in sunglasses said, “I'm not asking him about the parking,” and the person in front said, “It's a fair question,” and nobody explained, then or ever, what the parking was.

“Can I ask you something?” I said. “Who sends the texts?”

He thought about it, and seemed to decide I deserved an honest answer. “I really don't know, man. A number. Different every time. The money's real, though.” He looked up the hill at my house. “Nice place. It looks like a hotel.”

I had always thought so too. I had never said it to anyone.

I have been handed a great many things through car windows. Contracts. Keys. A check for a hospital wing, once, which I had to sign on the roof of the car because there was nowhere else. I had never been handed something I did not already understand.
