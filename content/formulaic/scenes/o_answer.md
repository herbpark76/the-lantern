## Malcolm

“Frank.”

It was his voice. It was older than I remembered and exactly as I remembered, dry and unhurried, the voice of a man who has never once been put on hold.

“Dad.”

“You're not at the office.”

“It's six fifty-two.”

“It is six fifty-two in Geneva also. I have been up since five.” A page turned. He read the *Financial Times* every morning at five, in a chair by the window, in a dressing gown he had owned since the Carter administration. “Your Indonesian business is on the wire. I would deal with it before the Americans wake up.”

I sat down on the floor of the hall. I had not sat on a floor since boarding school.

“Dad,” I said. “How are you?”

There was a silence on the line. I had never asked him that. In forty-three years it had not come up, the way some roads never come up on a map you use every day.

“Why?” he said.

“I'd like to know.”

Another page. “My knee,” he said finally. “The left. The man here wants to replace it, and I have told him I am not a car.”

“Is it raining there?”

“No.” He sounded, for the first time I could remember, faintly puzzled. “Why on earth would you ask me that?”

“No reason.”

“Composure, Frank,” my father said, and hung up, as he always had, without saying goodbye, as if a goodbye were a fee someone else ought to pay.

I sat on the floor of the hall in my pajamas and held the phone against my chest. From the bathroom Gabriella was singing the same eight notes over and over, getting them a little more right each time.
